0009 [Heading 1] Abstract
0010 [Abstract] Anxiety and depressive disorders impose a major global burden, motivating interest in non-pharmacological interventions that may modulate affective processes. Music exposure has been reported to influence anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, but preclinical findings are heterogeneous and have not been quantitatively synthesized. Most studies also focus on average responses, overlooking behavioral variability. We conducted a preregistered systematic review (Open Science Framework registration identifier: mv78t) and multilevel meta-analysis of experimental studies testing music exposure in laboratory rodents. Following PRISMA and PRISMA-EcoEvo guidelines, we synthesized 298 effect sizes from 20 studies using multilevel models that accounted for non-independence among effects. Average and variability effects were quantified using the log response ratio () and log variability ratio (), respectively. We also conducted a post hoc CRIME-Q appraisal of reporting quality, methodological quality, and risk of bias for all the included studies.Music exposure was associated with an average decrease of about 20% in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors relative to control conditions.. In contrast, music exposure did not produce an overall change in behavioral variability. Exploratory moderator analyses suggested context-dependent variability responses: behavioral assay type and music meta-genre accounted for comparatively greater heterogeneity in , with anxiety-like outcomes tending to show increased variability and depression-like outcomes tending to show reduced variability under music exposure.Critical appraisal identified frequent incomplete reporting of sample-size justification, allocation procedures, blinding, attrition, and key features of acoustic delivery and control conditions. Music exposure therefore appears to reliably shift average affect-related behavior without uniformly altering behavioral dispersion, but inference should be restricted primarily to short-term exposures in young adult laboratory rodents under controlled conditions.

0013 [First Paragraph] A synthesis of 20 rodent studies finds lower anxiety- and depression-like behaviors after music exposure, but substantial variation across experiments and reporting limitations.
0015 [Heading 1] 2	Introduction
0016 [First Paragraph] Anxiety and depressive disorders are among the most prevalent and disabling psychiatric conditions worldwide (Ferrari 2022; Airaksinen et al. 2024). Anxiety disorders are characterized by persistent fear, hypervigilance, and heightened stress reactivity, whereas depressive disorders involve sustained low mood, anhedonia, and impaired motivation. Together, these conditions affect hundreds of millions of people globally, causing a substantial economic burden and reducing quality of life (Ferrari 2022; Airaksinen et al. 2024; Ridley et al. 2020). Their high prevalence and chronic nature have driven extensive efforts to understand the neural mechanisms underlying affective dysregulation and to develop effective treatments. Pharmacological therapies remain a cornerstone of treatment, but their efficacy varies and they are often accompanied by side effects or incomplete symptom relief (Boulenger 2004; Kelly and Mezuk 2017). Consequently, interest has grown in complementary, non-pharmacological approaches that modulate affective states by engaging neural systems involved in emotion regulation and stress responsiveness. Among these, music exposure has received substantial attention (Witte et al. 2022). In humans, music reliably engages limbic, paralimbic, and cortical networks implicated in emotion, reward, and stress regulation, eliciting robust physiological and affective responses (Arnold et al. 2024; Koelsch 2014; Levitin and Tirovolas 2009). Clinical and experimental studies suggest that music can reduce self-reported anxiety and depressive symptoms, at least transiently, and may enhance emotional regulation across diverse contexts (Fancourt and Finn 2019; Thoma et al. 2013).
0017 [Body Text] Although the neural mechanisms through which music engages affective and stress-related systems are increasingly well characterized, translating these findings into reliable therapeutic applications remains challenging (Koelsch 2014; Fancourt et al. 2014). Individual differences in musical preference, prior experience, clinical condition, and cultural context introduce substantial variability in outcomes. This variability complicates assessments of when music exposure can consistently modulate anxiety- and depression-related processes (Balkwill and Thompson 1999; McDermott et al. 2016). Preclinical animal models provide a valuable framework to address these limitations. Rodent models allow precise control over auditory exposure, developmental timing, and environmental conditions, while enabling standardized assessments of anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. Behavioral assays such as the Elevated Plus Maze, Open Field Test, Light–Dark Box, Forced Swim Test, Tail Suspension Test, and Sucrose Preference Test are widely used to probe stress reactivity, avoidance behavior, and coping strategies under controlled conditions (e.g., Cryan and Holmes 2005; Slattery and Cryan 2012). These models permit systematic investigation of how environmental inputs interact with neural and endocrine systems implicated in affective regulation, including stress-related and motivational pathways (Mcewen et al. 2007; Nestler et al. 2002).
0018 [Body Text] Within this experimental framework, a growing literature has examined how music exposure affects rodent behavior. Many studies report reductions in anxiety- or depression-like behaviors following music exposure, consistent with the idea that temporally patterned auditory stimuli such as music can modulate stress-related processes (e.g., Escribano et al. 2014; Chikahisa et al. 2007). However, studies differ in the type of music used, exposure duration and timing, developmental stage at exposure, behavioral assays employed, control conditions, and whether behaviors are assessed under baseline conditions or following experimentally induced stress (Kühlmann et al. 2018; Le et al. 2025). Consequently, reported effects vary widely, leaving it unclear whether music exposure exerts a generalizable effect on affect-related behavior or whether its influence is highly context-dependent. A further limitation of the existing literature is its near-exclusive focus on changes in average behavioral responses. Yet behavioral variability is itself a biologically meaningful property (Westneat et al. 2015). Changes in variance can reflect altered stability, sensitivity to environmental perturbations, or heterogeneity in individual stress responsiveness. Environmental manipulations may reduce variability by stabilizing behavioral responses or increase variability by amplifying individual differences (Sih et al. 2015). Experimental evidence also shows that pharmacological exposures can alter behavioral variability itself: psychoactive compounds have been shown to suppress differences among individuals in fish behavior, including across different social contexts (Polverino et al. 2021; Rafeeq et al. 2026). Whether music exposure similarly affects behavioral variability alongside average behavior has not been systematically examined.
0019 [Body Text] Recent advances in meta-analytic methodology allow a more rigorous synthesis of this evidence. Multilevel meta-analytic models accommodate multiple, non-independent effect sizes from the same study while appropriately partitioning sources of variance (Nakagawa and Santos 2012). Effect size metrics such as the log response ratio () enable comparison across diverse behavioral measures, whereas variance-based metrics such as the log variability ratio () permit explicit evaluation of changes in behavioral variability (Nakagawa et al. 2015; Senior et al. 2020). Together, these approaches allow researchers to assess both the average effects of music exposure on affect-related behavior and the consistency of those effects across individuals and experimental contexts.
0020 [Body Text] Here, we present a preregistered systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis of experimental studies examining how music exposure affects anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in laboratory rodents. We focused on laboratory mice and rats experimentally exposed to music and compared with concurrent non-music controls, with eligibility restricted to studies reporting standardized anxiety- or depression-like behavioral outcomes. Reported in accordance with PRISMA and PRISMA-EcoEvo guidelines (O’Dea et al. 2021; Page et al. 2021), this synthesis quantifies both average behavioral effects and changes in behavioral variability, identifies sources of heterogeneity, and evaluates the robustness of reported effects. By clarifying the magnitude, consistency, and context dependence of music-induced behavioral changes under controlled conditions, this study provides a quantitative and conceptual framework for interpreting preclinical evidence relevant to affective regulation and hypotheses about sensory modulation.

0041 [Heading 2] 3.7	Risk of bias and reporting transparency
0042 [First Paragraph] We critically appraised 20 reports at the study level using the finalized CRIME-Q assessment (Fig. 8). All reports were peer reviewed, but reporting and methodological safeguards were uneven across items. Animal characteristics were fully reported in 10 studies (50%) and partly reported in 10 (50%); animal comparability and appropriateness were fully supported in only one study (5%), with the remaining 19 (95%) rated as Partly. No study reported an a priori sample-size calculation. The music-exposure intervention was described sufficiently for replication in 13 studies (65%) and partly in seven (35%). However, the technical quality of the music-exposure design was never rated fully satisfactory: 19 studies (95%) were rated Partly and one (5%) was rated No, mainly because of incomplete information on exposure fidelity, acoustic delivery, cage/litter independence, or control-condition design. A targeted review of the original exposure protocols further showed that music was delivered at the room level in 11 studies and at the cage level in four, while the exposure unit could not be determined confidently in five studies. No included study clearly used independently assigned individual-level music exposure. In 18 of 20 studies, multiple animals therefore shared a cage- or room-level acoustic environment while the primary statistical analyses treated animals as independent observations without explicitly accounting for cage, room, or litter structure. Several core risk-of-bias safeguards were poorly reported. Baseline balance was unclear in 16 studies (80%), allocation sequence generation in 19 (95%), and experimenter or handler blinding in 19 (95%). Random housing or protection against cage/position effects was unclear in 16 studies (80%) and absent in four (20%).
0043 [Body Text] Some domains were better reported. Outcome assessment followed a fixed or pre-specified rule in 19 studies (95%), methods and results were fully aligned in 16 (80%), attrition was adequately handled in 14 (70%), and selective outcome reporting was not evident in 19 (95%). Animal-welfare compliance was fully reported in 15 studies (75%) and partly in five (25%). Formal conflict-of-interest statements were present in 12 studies (60%), and inappropriate funder or commercial influence was judged absent in 13 (65%) but unclear in seven (35%). Overall, the CRIME-Q assessment identified substantial uncertainty arising from incomplete reporting of design safeguards, alongside a recurrent concern about treatment-level replication because acoustic exposure was commonly shared among multiple animals.
0044 [Heading 1] 4	Discussion
0045 [First Paragraph] Music exposure reduced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors by about 20% relative to control conditions across experimental contexts, as indicated by a significantly negative overall  (Fig. 3A). This reduction occurred across both anxiety- and depression-like outcomes (Fig. 4A) and across life stages at exposure (Fig. 5A), indicating that the overall direction of the association was generally consistent across these broad categories, despite substantial heterogeneity among effect sizes. However, the magnitude of the average response differed among behavioral assays, indicating that the estimated effect of music also depended on how affect-related behavior was measured. Although effect sizes showed substantial heterogeneity (Table 1), the predominance of negative effect sizes supports a clear directional effect. Music exposure did not systematically alter behavioral variability. The overall  estimate was close to zero and non-significant (Fig. 3B), showing that music exposure neither consistently reduced nor increased behavioral dispersion. In other words, music exposure shifted average affect-related behavior without broadly homogenizing responses or amplifying differences between individuals. This distinction highlights the conceptual difference between average effects (), which capture shifts in average behavior, and variability effects (), which capture changes in dispersion (Nakagawa et al. 2015). The absence of a global  effect indicates that music alters central tendency without uniformly reshaping individual variation. Exploratory outcome-specific analyses suggested different variability patterns for anxiety- and depression-like outcomes: variability tended to increase for anxiety-like behaviors and decrease for depression-like behaviors. However, the depression-specific evidence was strongly male biased, with female outcomes represented by only six effect sizes from two studies, so these differences should be interpreted cautiously. Moderator analyses also revealed potential heterogeneity in variability responses across behavioral assays, music meta-genres, and experimental designs (Fig. 6B–C). The estimated strain-level variance component was close to zero in both average and variability models. However, only six strains were represented in the dataset, and two of these occurred in a single study each. The near-zero estimate should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that strain has no biological influence on behavioral responses; rather, the available evidence provides limited information for estimating strain-level heterogeneity. This is particularly important because strain differences are known to influence behavioral outcomes (Russo et al. 2012)
0046 [Heading 2] 4.1	Music exposure in the context of environmental enrichment
0047 [First Paragraph] The direction of these average effects aligns with findings from other non-pharmacological interventions, particularly voluntary exercise and environmental enrichment. It is also broadly consistent with evidence from human studies reporting reductions in anxiety and depressive symptoms following music-based interventions (Witte et al. 2022), although the present results concern experimentally controlled exposure in laboratory rodents and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy in humans. Exercise reliably reduces immobility in stress-coping assays and increases exploratory behavior in anxiety-related tests (Duman et al. 2008; Mul and Mul 2018; Brenes et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2025). Although we cannot directly compare effect magnitudes across paradigms, the shared direction of effects suggests that music functions as a sensory form of environmental enrichment rather than as a pharmacological analogue. Music exposure produced stronger reductions in induced paradigms than in assays measuring baseline affective behavior (Fig. 5D). Induced models (e.g., chronic stress or learned helplessness) elevate anxiety- and depression-like phenotypes above typical levels (Mineur et al. 2006), creating greater scope for behavioral improvement. Interventions that modulate stress physiology should therefore produce larger effects under heightened disturbance than under baseline conditions, where responses already fall within a restricted range.
0048 [Body Text] Timing also influenced effect magnitude. Studies that delivered music prior to behavioral testing showed larger reductions in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors than studies presenting music concurrently with testing. Rather than contradicting evidence for acute music-related changes in arousal or cognition (Rickard et al. 2005), this pattern suggests that music primarily shifts baseline affective states rather than enhancing task performance. When introduced during testing, music may act as an additional sensory stimulus that interacts with task demands, whereas prior exposure allows physiological and affective modulation to occur before assessment. Several mechanisms may account for these patterns. Music may modulate stress physiology through the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, reducing stress reactivity and downstream glucocorticoid signaling (Smith and Vale 2006; Mcewen et al. 2007). Alternatively, music may increase environmental predictability. Chronic unpredictable mild stress reliably induces anxiety- and depression-like phenotypes (Willner 2017), whereas music exposure typically occurs in stable, repeatable contexts. By increasing sensory predictability, music may dampen stress responses associated with uncertainty. This interpretation aligns with the joint – pattern: music consistently shifts average behavior while altering variability only in specific contexts. As with other enrichment-based interventions, heterogeneity is expected rather than anomalous (Würbel 2001; Simpson and Kelly 2011).
0049 [Heading 2] 4.2	Assay-, stimulus-, and design-specific variability
0050 [First Paragraph] Behavioral assays strongly moderated variability responses. Relative to the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM), music exposure reduced variability in the Forced Swim Test and Tail Suspension Test (Fig. 6A). These assays constrain animals to a limited behavioral repertoire and probe stress-coping states (Cryan and Holmes 2005; Leite-Almeida et al. 2022). Under such constraints, music appears to promote convergence toward similar coping responses, reducing behavioral dispersion across animals. In contrast, music exposure increased variability in the EPM. The EPM assesses approach–avoidance conflict and typically produces substantial individual variability (Walf and Frye 2007; Knight et al. 2021; Albani et al. 2015). Music may amplify pre-existing differences in how animals balance exploration and avoidance, increasing dispersion even when average anxiety-like behavior declines. Increased variability in this context does not imply adverse effects; rather, it reflects the sensitivity of this behavioral domain to individual traits and assay structure.
0051 [Body Text] These findings carry practical implications. Researchers who prioritize reproducibility and precise estimation of average effects may obtain more consistent outcomes using assays that exhibit lower variability under music exposure, such as the Forced Swim Test or Tail Suspension Test. In contrast, assays like the EPM may require larger samples or explicit modeling of heterogeneity to achieve comparable precision. No assay is inherently superior; instead, tasks differ in how consistently animals respond under identical auditory conditions.
0052 [Body Text] Music meta-genre also structured variability. Studies using popular contemporary and traditional or folk music showed greater dispersion than studies using mixed stimuli, whereas Western art or orchestral music did not differ significantly from the reference category (Fig. 6C). Although genre categories are coarse, they likely capture differences in auditory temporal structure and predictability. Predictable auditory patterns tend to elicit more consistent neural responses (Heilbron and Chait 2018; Auksztulewicz et al. 2017), whereas less predictable rhythms amplify differential processing and expectation violations (Foldal et al. 2020). Thus, genre-dependent variability likely reflects differences in how consistently animals encode and integrate auditory input.
0053 [Body Text] Experimental design also influenced variability. Posttest-only designs were associated with greater behavioral dispersion under music exposure than factorial or repeated-measures designs (Fig. 6D), as well as more negative average effects relative to factorial designs. Thus, studies using posttest-only designs tended to report larger reductions in anxiety- and depression-like behavior, but also a greater music–control difference in behavioral variability. One possible explanation is that more structured designs can better isolate music-specific effects from variation associated with baseline states, time, co-manipulations, or repeated testing. By contrast, posttest-only comparisons may retain these unmeasured sources of variation within the observed treatment contrast.
0054 [Body Text] Design architecture may therefore affect both the estimated average effect and the apparent dispersion response, rather than merely acting as a neutral feature of study implementation. However, this moderator pattern should not be interpreted causally; design architecture is likely confounded by other experimental features, including the choice of induced model, exposure protocol, and behavioral assay. Nevertheless, it indicates that some variability attributed to music exposure may reflect differences in experimental design rather than genuine biological heterogeneity in individual responses alone.
0055 [Heading 2] 4.3	Heterogeneity, reporting practices, and robustness
0056 [First Paragraph] The combined  and  results indicate substantial heterogeneity in how music exposure was implemented and experienced across studies (Kühlmann et al. 2018; Le et al. 2025). Differences in genre, duration, timing, acoustic delivery, control conditions, and experimental context are likely to interact in non-additive ways, complicating mechanistic inference. Music meta-genre explained little heterogeneity in average effects but substantial heterogeneity in behavioral variability, indicating that average outcomes alone can obscure important dimensions of behavioral sensitivity.
0057 [Body Text] The CRIME-Q assessment (Fig. [fig]) is most informative not as an inventory of missing information, but as a limit on causal attribution. In an acoustic intervention, the nominal stimulus does not fully define the treatment received by the animals. Sound level and frequency spectrum, speaker placement, room acoustics, cage position, background noise, group housing, and separation from control animals can all modify effective exposure. Ambient acoustic conditions can vary substantially among rooms and cages, and differences in acoustic context can alter rodent behavior even when other experimental procedures are similar (Prior 2006; Barabas et al. 2022). Consequently, incomplete reporting of exposure delivery and control conditions makes it difficult to determine whether differences among studies reflect the musical stimulus itself, total acoustic stimulation, or broader features of the housing environment.
0058 [Body Text] Similarly, unclear ratings for baseline balance, allocation, housing, and blinding do not demonstrate that the studies were biased; rather, they limit our ability to evaluate the direction and magnitude of any bias (Hooijmans et al. 2014; Andersen et al. 2024). A more specific concern emerged for treatment-level replication. Our review of the original exposure protocols showed that music was usually delivered to several animals sharing a cage or room, whereas primary analyses generally treated individual animals as independent replicates. In such designs, the cage or room rather than the individual animal may constitute the independently replicated treatment unit. Because our sampling variances were calculated from the animal sample sizes reported by the original studies, any unaccounted cage- or room-level clustering is inherited by the meta-analysis. Where the number of animals exceeds the number of independent acoustic exposure units, sampling variances may therefore be underestimated and individual effect sizes may receive greater precision than warranted. Cage- and room-level replication was not reported consistently enough to reconstruct effective sample sizes retrospectively, so this limitation could not be corrected quantitatively. Thus, the observed heterogeneity likely reflects a combination of biological differences, methodological variation, and differences in experimental structure and precision across studies rather than any single source.
0059 [Body Text] Despite these limitations, multiple diagnostics support the robustness of the overall average effect: we found no evidence of small-study or time-lag bias, and leave-one-out analyses showed that no individual study drove the estimated effect. These diagnostics indicate that the pooled result was not attributable to a single influential study or a clear publication-related artifact, although they do not address the treatment-level replication issue described above. Generalization should nevertheless be restricted to the experimental contexts represented in the literature. Most studies involved young adult laboratory mice or rats exposed to music over relatively short periods under controlled housing conditions. The estimated  reduction in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors should therefore be interpreted as applying primarily to comparable laboratory settings. Extrapolation to other developmental stages, longer exposures, other species, or more complex environments requires direct testing.
0060 [Body Text] Other design features also remain insufficiently characterized. Reporting of the light–dark phase during music exposure or behavioral testing was uncommon, limiting assessment of whether circadian timing contributed to variation in either average effects or behavioral variability (Saré et al. 2021). In addition, most primary studies were designed to detect average differences rather than changes in behavioral variability. The context-dependent  patterns should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the smaller evidence base and exploratory nature of the moderator analyses.
0061 [Heading 2] 4.4	Study-specific limitations and recommendations for future research
0062 [First Paragraph] The CRIME-Q assessment identifies several priorities for future research on music exposure and affect-related behavior. Studies should report music and control conditions in sufficient detail to permit replication, including the stimulus identity or composition, delivery hardware, sound level, speaker–cage arrangement, exposure schedule, acoustic separation between treatment and control animals, and whether exposure was delivered at the cage, room, or animal level. Reporting the light–dark phase of exposure and testing, habituation procedures, housing conditions, and cage or room allocation would further clarify potential circadian and environmental sources of variation. Future experiments should also strengthen safeguards against bias by reporting a priori sample-size calculations, explicit allocation methods, baseline group characteristics, housing or position randomization, blinding of handlers and outcome assessors where feasible, and animal-level attrition and exclusions by treatment group. Prespecifying outcomes and making protocols or analysis plans available would improve the assessment of selective reporting. Finally, studies should be designed to assess both average responses and behavioral variability. This will require larger samples, repeated-measures designs where appropriate, and reporting of individual-level data or response distributions rather than group summaries alone. The experimental unit should be specified explicitly, with cage- or room-level replication and hierarchical analyses used when exposure or housing is shared among animals. Together, these improvements would allow future work to distinguish more clearly between music-specific effects, broader acoustic or housing effects, and genuine differences in individual behavioral responsiveness.
0063 [Heading 2] 4.5	Conclusions
0064 [First Paragraph] This meta-analysis shows that music exposure is associated, on average, with an approximately 20% reduction in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in laboratory rodents, while effects on behavioral variability are strongly context dependent. Music exposure primarily shifts the central tendency of affect-related behavior, with changes in behavioral dispersion emerging only under specific experimental and stimulus conditions. Joint consideration of average effects and variability therefore provides a more complete framework for interpreting heterogeneous experimental results and for evaluating sensory and environmental interventions.
0065 [Heading 1] 5	Materials and Methods
0066 [Heading 2] 5.1	Literature searching
0067 [First Paragraph] We used the PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome; Table 2) framework (Foo et al. 2021; Rickard et al. 2005) to define the scope of the review and guide literature searching. We searched Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and OpenAlex. OpenAlex was included to improve coverage of newly indexed and non-traditionally published records that may not yet be fully integrated into subscription-based databases. Our search strings targeted experimental studies examining the effects of music exposure on anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in laboratory rodents. No temporal restrictions were applied to publication dates. We provide database-specific search strings in the Supplementary Material (S1). We evaluated search sensitivity by estimating relative recall against a benchmark set of 10 pre-identified experimental studies (Lagisz et al. 2025).
0068 [Body Text] To enhance coverage and identify gray literature, we additionally searched Google Scholar and the Bielefeld Academic Search Engine (BASE). Search strings were translated into Spanish, Japanese, Polish, and Russian, and Google Scholar screening was restricted to the first 50 results per language query. 0071 [Heading 2] 5.2	Eligibility criteria and screening
0072 [First Paragraph] Study eligibility criteria followed the preregistered protocol and PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome; Table 3) framework. In brief, to be eligible for inclusion, studies had to report experiments assessing the effects of music exposure on anxiety- or depression-like behaviors in laboratory mice or rats. We excluded studies using wild-caught animals. For genetically modified models or animals subjected to invasive interventions (e.g., surgical or pharmacological procedures), we only included data from wild-type or sham-operated groups, if available; valid comparisons were restricted to music-exposed versus control animals within the same genetic or procedural background. Studies relying exclusively on physiological or histological outcomes without standardized behavioral assays were excluded. We also excluded studies lacking a concurrent control group because treatment effects could not be estimated without a comparator. We used Rayyan (Ouzzani et al. 2016) for screening titles and abstracts from bibliographic records. English-language records were screened independently by at least two reviewers (S.O., M.L., A.L., A.M., E.L., S.N., and S.P.), whereas non-English records were screened by a single reviewer with relevant language expertise. Full-text screening followed the same procedure, also in Rayyan. Screening of non-English language records and studies was conducted by reviewers with relevant language expertise (e.g., S.P., A.L., and S.O. for Spanish; M.L. for Polish and Russian; A.M. for Japanese).
0073 [Heading 2] 5.3	Data collection
0074 [First Paragraph] We extracted four categories of data from each eligible study. Data extraction was conducted by S.O., and a random 10% subset of studies (two studies) was independently cross-checked for accuracy against the original reports. We extracted: (i) Bibliographic information (title, authors, year, source, DOI). (ii) Music exposure and experimental conditions, including music type and, when reported, specific piece/composer; exposure duration (hours/day) and total exposure length (days); timing relative to behavioral testing (before, concurrent, or both); and light–dark phase during exposure; acoustic characteristics (sound level in dB; frequency in Hz) when available; control conditions (silence, ambient sound, or white noise) and their acoustic properties when reported; additional experimental manipulations (e.g., housing or stress procedures) when applied equivalently across treatment and control groups.(iii) We extracted numerical behavioral outcome data needed for effect size computation, including the number of animals contributing to each control and music-exposed condition, group means, and measures of dispersion (SD, SE, or confidence intervals). These animal counts were used as the reported sample sizes for effect-size calculation and do not necessarily correspond to the number of independently replicated exposure units, because music was often delivered to multiple animals sharing a cage or room.Outcomes were drawn from standardized behavioral assays only (e.g., Elevated Plus Maze, Open Field Test, Light–Dark Box, Forced Swim Test, Tail Suspension Test, Sucrose Preference Test). When numerical values were reported only graphically, we extracted data from plots using the metaDigitise R package (Pick et al. 2019). Outcomes were classified as reflecting innate behavior (e.g., approach–avoidance to exposed, well-lit spaces) or behavior induced by experimental procedures (e.g., chronic unpredictable mild stress) (Cryan and Holmes 2005). (iv) Study population characteristics, including species, strain, sex, age at exposure/testing, randomization, blinding, sham procedures, attrition reporting, and completeness of outcome reporting. When information critical for effect size calculation was unclear—most commonly when sample sizes were reported as ranges rather than exact values—we contacted study authors by email (two studies) to request clarification. We received no responses to our emails, and thus we used the midpoint of the reported sample-size range for effect size computation.
0075 [Heading 2] 5.4	Deviations from the preregistered protocol
0076 [First Paragraph] We implemented six refinements to our protocol after preregistration. To accommodate heterogeneity in reporting practices, we introduced the first three refinements during data extraction, whereas the final two were added as post hoc assessments. Crucially, these adjustments did not alter the PECO framework, eligibility criteria, outcome definitions, or primary analytical strategy.
0077 [Body Text] First, to model statistical dependence among effect sizes, we coded the comparison structure of each effect size as either independent or dependent (within-subject/repeated-measures). This expansion of our preregistered metadata structure allowed for accurate sampling-variance specification when a single experimental cohort yielded multiple effect sizes.
0078 [Body Text] Second, to harmonize heterogeneous reporting, we classified exposure durations (acute, short-term, medium-term, or long-term) and grouped music stimuli into broad meta-genres based on historical context and dominant musical features (see Table S1 for definitions). We derived these classifications from the reported text to facilitate interpretable moderator analyses.
0079 [Body Text] Third, we recorded whether studies explicitly reported key stimulus parameters, such as loudness, frequency, and light–dark phases. Because loudness and frequency were frequently under-reported, we explicitly coded instances of non-reporting instead of treating them as missing data, allowing us to quantify reporting gaps.
0080 [Body Text] Fourth, we introduced a post hoc critical appraisal of the included evidence using the Critical Appraisal of Methodological (technical) Quality, Quality of Reporting and Risk of Bias in Animal Research (CRIME-Q) framework (Andersen et al. 2024). We evaluated each eligible study against 20 item-level criteria spanning quality of reporting, methodological quality, and risk of bias.
0081 [Body Text] Fifth, following concerns about the level of treatment replication, we conducted a post hoc targeted review of the original exposure protocols to determine whether music was delivered at the animal, cage, or room level and whether the primary analyses accounted for shared exposure or housing structure. This assessment was used to evaluate the potential for pseudoreplication and overestimation of precision arising when multiple animals shared the same acoustic exposure but were analyzed as independent observations.
0082 [Body Text]  Sixth, we conducted an additional post hoc sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the results to the correlation assumptions used to model non-independence. We varied both the within-subject correlation used for dependent comparisons () and the correlation among sampling errors of multiple effect sizes from the same cohort () across values of 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 0.90, both separately and jointly. We then examined whether the overall  and  estimates, and the anxiety–depression contrast in , changed across these assumptions.

0100 [First Paragraph] Differences in behavioral variability were quantified using the (bias-corrected) log variability ratio (Nakagawa et al. 2015; Senior et al. 2020). We therefore did not interpret differences in SD for proportional outcomes as direct evidence of changes in behavioral variability.

0110 [Heading 2] 5.6	Meta-analytic models
0111 [First Paragraph] We fitted multilevel meta-analytic models separately for  and  using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation in metafor (Viechtbauer 2010). Because individual studies often contributed multiple, non-independent effect sizes (e.g., multiple outcomes, assays, or contrasts from the same experimental cohort), we explicitly modeled dependence among effect sizes in two ways. First, sampling covariances were incorporated using a variance–covariance matrix () constructed at the cohort level, such that effect sizes derived from the same cohort were assumed to have correlated sampling errors (with  when within-cohort correlations were not reported), yielding a block-diagonal-structured  matrix (Noble et al. 2017; Nakagawa et al. 2022). Second, we included random intercepts for study ID to account for between-study heterogeneity, for effect size ID to accommodate residual within-study variation among effect sizes beyond sampling error, and for strain to control for systematic differences among rodent strains. Moderator analyses were conducted as separate uni-moderator meta-regressions using the same random-effects structure and the same  matrix as the corresponding overall model. Given the number of moderators and pairwise contrasts relative to the size of the evidence base, these analyses were treated as exploratory. We did not apply a formal multiple-comparison correction because stringent family-wise error corrections can substantially reduce statistical power and increase the risk of false-negative results when many related hypotheses are evaluated (Pike 2011; Drezner and Drezner 2016, Gelman et al. 2012). Instead, we considered the magnitude and uncertainty of estimated effects together with the proportion of heterogeneity explained by each moderator, and interpreted individual contrasts as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Inference was based on -tests as implemented in metafor, and all models were fitted using REML.

0120 [Heading 2] 5.8	Small-study effects, time-lag patterns, and sensitivity analyzes
0121 [First Paragraph] We assessed potential small-study effects and time-lag patterns using complementary visual and model-based approaches appropriate for multilevel meta-analyzes (Nakagawa et al. 2022). These analyzes were treated as diagnostic tools and interpreted cautiously given the substantial heterogeneity and non-independence of effect sizes. First, we visually inspected funnel plots for asymmetry using effect sizes from the fitted multilevel meta-analytic model, plotted against measures of precision (standard error and inverse standard error). Visual inspection was used to assess whether less precise studies showed systematic deviations from the overall pattern. Second, we evaluated small-study effects using an Egger-type regression adapted for multilevel meta-analytic models. In classical Egger’s regression, effect sizes are regressed on their sampling standard errors (Egger et al. 1997). However, for the log response ratio (), effect sizes and sampling variances are inherently correlated, which can bias inference. To avoid this issue, we used the effective sample size:

This term was included as a moderator in multilevel meta-regression models with the same random-effects structure as the primary analyzes. Third, we examined potential time-lag (decline) effects by including mean-centered publication year as a moderator. To disentangle temporal trends from small-study effects, we fitted both uni-moderator models (including either publication year or ) and a multi-moderator model including both predictors simultaneously. All models followed the same variance–covariance structure and random-effects specification as the primary meta-analyzes (Nakagawa et al. 2022). Finally, we assessed the influence of individual studies using a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Each study was sequentially removed from the dataset, and the multilevel meta-analytic model was refitted to evaluate the stability of the overall effect estimate. This analysis was used to identify whether any single study exerted disproportionate influence on the estimated average effect or its uncertainty (Nakagawa, Yang, et al. 2023).
0124 [Heading 2] 5.9	Critical appraisal of reporting, methodological quality, and risk of bias
0125 [First Paragraph] After the primary analyses, we conducted a post hoc critical appraisal using an adapted version of the Critical Appraisal of Methodological (technical) Quality, Quality of Reporting and Risk of Bias in Animal Research (CRIME-Q) framework (Andersen et al. 2024). We assessed 20 reports at the study level using 20 items across three domains: quality of reporting (QoR), methodological quality (MQ), and risk of bias (RoB). We omitted CRIME-Q bench-top or laboratory-procedure items that were not applicable to these whole-animal music-exposure experiments.
0126 [Body Text] QoR items evaluated whether reports provided sufficient detail for replication and appraisal, including peer-review status, animal characteristics, music-exposure procedures, animal-welfare compliance, blinding, methods–results congruency, study limitations, and conflicts of interest. MQ items evaluated whether the animals and music-exposure design were appropriate for the stated behavioral question, including group comparability, sample-size justification, acoustic delivery, exposure fidelity, control conditions, and potential acoustic cross-contamination. RoB items examined baseline balance (whether relevant pre-intervention animal or group characteristics were comparable between music and control groups), allocation sequence generation, housing or cage-position effects, outcome-assessment selection rules, experimenter or handler blinding, outcome-assessor blinding, attrition, selective outcome reporting, and potential funder or commercial influence.
0127 [Body Text] Each item was scored using its prespecified CRIME-Q response options. Reporting and methodological-quality items were generally scored as Yes, Partly, or No; risk-of-bias items were generally scored as Yes, No, or Unclear, with Not applicable available only for items where the codebook allowed it. We retained ratings as item-level categorical data and did not combine them into an overall quality score. S.O. performed the initial appraisal using the original reports and a study-specific CRIME-Q coding manual. NotebookLM (3.5 Flash) was then used as a source-grounded extraction aid to cross-check each item against the primary studies, the adapted CRIME-Q codebook, and the coding rules. The AI output was treated as provisional: all discrepancies and uncertain ratings were resolved by rechecking the original sources. Finally, A.L. independently cross-checked a random sample of two studies (10% of reports). The adapted CRIME-Q codebook, including the item definitions, response options, and operational decision rules used in the appraisal, is provided in Supplementary Material. The NotebookLM prompts and instructions, supporting workflow files, and finalized study-level assessment matrix are available in the project repository at  https://github.com/Santiago-0rtega/Music_on_anxiety_depression_meta_analysis.

Moderator | Definition and coding
Life stage at exposure | Age category at music exposure: Neonatal (birth–1 week), Infantile (1–2 weeks), Juvenile (2–4 weeks), Adolescent/Peripubertal (4–8 weeks), Young adult (2–5 months), Adult (5–18 months), or Old (18 months). Multiple ages were coded as Mixed; missing or unclear information as Not reported or Unclear.
Experimental design | Posttest-only (independent treatment and control groups measured after exposure), Repeated-measures (the same animals assessed across time or conditions), or Factorial (music exposure crossed with one or more additional experimental factors).
Behavioral mechanism | Behavior measured under baseline conditions (Innate) or following an experimental procedure intended to induce anxiety- or depression-like phenotypes (Induced; e.g., chronic stress or maternal separation).
Experimental procedure | Whether a sham procedure was used to control for an additional surgical or pharmacological manipulation. Coded as None or Sham.
Relative timing | Timing of music exposure relative to behavioral testing: Before, Concurrent, Both (before and during testing), or Not specified.
Exposure duration | Total period of music exposure: Acute (1–7 days), Short-term (8–28 days), Medium-term (29–60 days), or Long-term (>60 days).
Music meta-genre | Broad classification of the music stimulus: Western art/orchestral, Popular contemporary (post-1950 mainstream styles), Traditional/folk/world, Mixed (multiple genres), or Unclear.
Control condition | Acoustic condition experienced by control animals: Silence, Ambient sound, or White noise.
Outcome type | Behavioral construct represented by the extracted outcome: Anxiety, Depression, Both, or Unclear. Anxiety-like outcomes included measures of exploration or avoidance in assays such as the Elevated Plus Maze, Open Field Test, and Light–Dark Box, whereas depression-like outcomes included measures from assays such as the Forced Swim Test, Tail Suspension Test, and Sucrose Preference Test.
