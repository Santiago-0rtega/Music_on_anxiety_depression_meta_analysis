0002 [Normal]  Overall comments: This study (bio.062879) presents a preregistered systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis of experimental studies examining how music exposure affects anxiety- and depression-like behaviours in laboratory rodents. Using ~ 300 effect sizes from 20 studies, the authors quantify both mean effects (using lnRR) and changes in behavioural variability (using lnVR), test a range of moderators, assess publication bias, and conduct a CRIME-Q appraisal of the included studies. They report an average reduction of ~ 20% in anxiety- and depression-like behaviours, no overall change in behavioural variability, and several context-dependent moderator effects. Overall, I thought this manuscript was really well done. I found it to be a clear, careful, and methodologically thorough. I particularly appreciated the preregistration, the joint modelling of mean and variability effects, and the honest CRIME-Q appraisal, which the authors use appropriately as a limit on causal attribution. The manuscript is also refreshingly well-calibrated in its claims, and the generalisation is sensibly restricted to the contexts actually represented in the literature. I have only a handful of comments, most of them minor, which I hope the authors find useful in revising the manuscript. My main substantive concern is around the number of moderators tested relative to the size of the evidence base, which I expand on below.

0034 [Normal] ● My overall main comment is about the moderator analyses. You test a large number of moderators (behavioural assay, mechanism, age, control condition, timing, meta-genre, design, sex, duration, procedure, outcome type) univariately, on both lnRR and lnVR, and report a number of pairwise contrasts. Many of the "significant" contrasts sit at p-values close to 0.05, and are drawn from relatively small subsets of a 20-study evidence base. With this many tests, did you perform any corrections multiple comparisons? If not, I would be somewhat more cautious about how much weight is placed on any individual contrast. I do not think you need to run a full multi-moderator model if the data are too sparse to support it, but I would encourage you to temper the language around the individual moderator contrasts so readers treat them as more hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.
0035 [Normal] We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. We did not apply a formal multiple-comparison correction. We have now made this explicit in the Methods and explain that, although such corrections can reduce false-positive findings, stringent family-wise corrections can also substantially reduce statistical power and increase the risk of false-negative results when many related hypotheses are evaluated (Pike, 2011; Drezner & Drezner, 2016, Gelman et al. 2012). Given this trade-off, and the number of moderators and pairwise contrasts relative to the size of the evidence base, we treat these analyses as exploratory rather than confirmatory. We therefore interpret individual contrasts in terms of their effect magnitude, uncertainty, and the proportion of heterogeneity explained, and explicitly describe them as hypothesis-generating.

0042 [Normal] ● Lines 203 - 204: "dispersion across individuals". If the underlying study has used repeated measure design, would ln VR not be a composite measure of both changes to among and within individual behavioural variation?
0043 [Normal] We thank the reviewer for raising this point. In the repeated-measures studies included here, the reported SDs describe the dispersion of animals at each condition or measurement occasion, rather than a decomposition of within- and among-individual behavioral variance. Thus, ln VR still compares behavioral dispersion between conditions. The within-individual correlation is used to account for the dependence between repeated observations when calculating the sampling variance of ln VR, rather than being incorporated into the effect size as a separate within-individual variance component. We have clarified this distinction in the Methods (lines 667--670).
