0014 Music exposure may influence affect-related behavior in rodents, but results vary across studies, and effects
0015 on behavioral variability remain unclear. We conducted a preregistered systematic review and multilevel
0016 meta-analysis of 20 experimental studies (298 effect sizes) testing music exposure in laboratory rodents.
0017 Using the log response ratio (lnRR) and log variability ratio (lnVR), we estimated changes in average anxiety-
0018 and depression-like behavior and behavioral dispersion while accounting for non-independence among
0019 effects. We also assessed reporting, methodological quality, and risk of bias using CRIME-Q. Music exposure
0020 was associated with an approximately 20% reduction in anxiety- and depression-like behavior relative to
0021 control conditions, with no overall change in behavioral variability. Exploratory moderator analyses
0022 suggested context-dependent dispersion: anxiety-like outcomes tended toward greater variability and
0023 depression-like outcomes toward lower variability, while behavioral assay and music meta-genre explained
0024 comparatively more heterogeneity in lnVR. Reporting of sample-size justification, allocation, blinding,
0025 attrition, and acoustic delivery was often incomplete. The average reduction applies primarily to the studied
0026 laboratory contexts; variability effects and generalization beyond predominantly young adult rodents and
0027 short-term exposure require caution.
0028 Keywords— anxiety; depression; rodent models; auditory enrichment; stress reactivity;
0029 behavioral variability; multilevel meta-analysis

0002 Introduction
0034 
0035 Anxiety and depressive disorders are among the most prevalent and disabling psychiatric
0036 conditions worldwide (Ferrari 2022; Spytska 2024). Anxiety disorders are characterized by
0037 persistent fear, hypervigilance, and heightened stress reactivity, whereas depressive
0038 disorders involve sustained low mood, anhedonia, and impaired motivation. Together,
0039 these conditions affect hundreds of millions of people globally, causing a substantial
0040 economic burden and reducing quality of life (Ferrari 2022; Spytska 2024; Ridley et al.
0041 2020). Their high prevalence and chronic nature have driven extensive efforts to
0042 understand the neural mechanisms underlying affective dysregulation and to develop
0043 effective treatments. Pharmacological therapies remain a cornerstone of treatment, but
0044 their efficacy varies and they are often accompanied by side effects or incomplete
0045 symptom relief (Boulenger 2004; Kelly and Mezuk 2017). Consequently, interest has grown
0046 in complementary, non-pharmacological approaches that modulate affective states by
0047 engaging neural systems involved in emotion regulation and stress responsiveness. Among
0048 these, music exposure has received substantial attention (Witte et al. 2022). In humans,
0049 music reliably engages limbic, paralimbic, and cortical networks implicated in emotion,
0050 reward, and stress regulation, eliciting robust physiological and affective responses
0051 (Arnold et al. 2024; Koelsch 2014; Levitin and Tirovolas 2009). Clinical and experimental
0052 studies suggest that music can reduce self-reported anxiety and depressive symptoms, at
0053 least transiently, and may enhance emotional regulation across diverse contexts (Fancourt
0054 and Finn 2019; Thoma et al. 2013).
0055 Although the neural mechanisms through which music engages affective and stress-
0056 related systems are increasingly well characterized, translating these findings into reliable
0057 therapeutic applications remains challenging (Koelsch 2014; Fancourt et al. 2014).
0058 Individual differences in musical preference, prior experience, clinical condition, and
0059 cultural context introduce substantial variability in outcomes. This variability complicates
0060 assessments of when music exposure can consistently modulate anxiety- and depression-
0061 related processes (Balkwill and Thompson 1999; McDermott et al. 2016). Preclinical
0003 
0062 animal models provide a valuable framework to address these limitations. Rodent models
0063 allow precise control over auditory exposure, developmental timing, and environmental
0064 conditions, while enabling standardized assessments of anxiety- and depression-like
0065 behaviors. Behavioral assays such as the Elevated Plus Maze, Open Field Test, Light–Dark
0066 Box, Forced Swim Test, Tail Suspension Test, and Sucrose Preference Test are widely used
0067 to probe stress reactivity, avoidance behavior, and coping strategies under controlled
0068 conditions (e.g., Cryan and Holmes 2005; Slattery and Cryan 2012). These models permit
0069 systematic investigation of how environmental inputs interact with neural and endocrine
0070 systems implicated in affective regulation, including stress-related and motivational
0071 pathways (Mcewen et al. 2007; Nestler et al. 2002).
0072 Within this experimental framework, a growing literature has examined how music
0073 exposure affects rodent behavior. Many studies report reductions in anxiety- or
0074 depression-like behaviors following music exposure, consistent with the idea that
0075 temporally patterned auditory stimuli such as music can modulate stress-related
0076 processes (e.g., Escribano et al. 2014; Chikahisa et al. 2007). However, studies differ in the
0077 type of music used, exposure duration and timing, developmental stage at exposure,
0078 behavioral assays employed, control conditions, and whether behaviors are assessed
0079 under baseline conditions or following experimentally induced stress (Kühlmann et al.
0080 2018; Le et al. 2025). Consequently, reported effects vary widely, leaving it unclear
0081 whether music exposure exerts a generalizable effect on affect-related behavior or
0082 whether its influence is highly context-dependent. A further limitation of the existing
0083 literature is its near-exclusive focus on changes in average behavioral responses. Yet
0084 behavioral variability is itself a biologically meaningful property (Westneat et al. 2015).
0085 Changes in variance can reflect altered stability, sensitivity to environmental perturbations,
0086 or heterogeneity in individual stress responsiveness. Environmental manipulations may
0087 reduce variability by stabilizing behavioral responses or increase variability by amplifying
0088 individual differences (Sih et al. 2015). Experimental evidence also shows that
0089 pharmacological exposures can alter behavioral variability itself: psychoactive
0090 compounds have been shown to suppress differences among individuals in fish behavior,
0004 
0091 including across different social contexts (Polverino et al. 2021; Rafeeq et al. 2026).
0092 Whether music exposure similarly affects behavioral variability alongside average behavior
0093 has not been systematically examined.
0094 Recent advances in meta-analytic methodology allow a more rigorous synthesis of this
0095 evidence. Multilevel meta-analytic models accommodate multiple, non-independent
0096 effect sizes from the same study while appropriately partitioning sources of variance
0097 (Nakagawa and Santos 2012). Effect size metrics such as the log response ratio (ln𝑅𝑅)
0098 enable comparison across diverse behavioral measures, whereas variance-based metrics
0099 such as the log variability ratio (ln𝑉𝑅) permit explicit evaluation of changes in behavioral
0100 variability (Nakagawa et al. 2015; Senior et al. 2020). Together, these approaches allow
0101 researchers to assess both the average effects of music exposure on affect-related
0102 behavior and the consistency of those effects across individuals and experimental
0103 contexts.
0104 Here, we present a preregistered systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis of
0105 experimental studies examining how music exposure affects anxiety- and depression-like
0106 behaviors in laboratory rodents. We focused on laboratory mice and rats experimentally
0107 exposed to music and compared with concurrent non-music controls, with eligibility
0108 restricted to studies reporting standardized anxiety- or depression-like behavioral
0109 outcomes. Reported in accordance with PRISMA and PRISMA-EcoEvo guidelines (O’Dea et
0110 al. 2021; Page et al. 2021), this synthesis quantifies both average behavioral effects and
0111 changes in behavioral variability, identifies sources of heterogeneity, and evaluates the
0112 robustness of reported effects. By clarifying the magnitude, consistency, and context
0113 dependence of music-induced behavioral changes under controlled conditions, this study
0114 provides a quantitative and conceptual framework for interpreting preclinical evidence
0115 relevant to affective regulation and hypotheses about sensory modulation.

0255 3.7  Risk of bias and reporting transparency
0256 We critically appraised 20 reports at the study level using the finalized CRIME-Q
0257 assessment (Fig. 7). All reports were peer reviewed, but reporting and methodological
0258 safeguards were uneven across items. Animal characteristics were fully reported in 10
0259 studies (50%) and partly reported in 10 (50%); animal comparability and appropriateness
0260 were fully supported in only one study (5%), with the remaining 19 (95%) rated as Partly. No
0261 study reported an a priori sample-size calculation. The music-exposure intervention was
0262 described sufficiently for replication in 13 studies (65%) and partly in seven (35%).
0263 However, the technical quality of the music-exposure design was never rated fully
0264 satisfactory: 19 studies (95%) were rated Partly and one (5%) was rated No, mainly
0265 because of incomplete information on exposure fidelity, acoustic delivery, cage/litter
0266 independence, or control-condition design. A targeted review of the original exposure
0014 
0267 protocols further showed that music was delivered at the room level in 11 studies and at
0268 the cage level in four, while the exposure unit could not be determined confidently in five
0269 studies. No included study clearly used independently assigned individual-level music
0270 exposure. In 18 of 20 studies, multiple animals therefore shared a cage- or room-level
0271 acoustic environment while the primary statistical analyses treated animals as
0272 independent observations without explicitly accounting for cage, room, or litter structure.
0273 Several core risk-of-bias safeguards were poorly reported. Baseline balance was unclear in
0274 16 studies (80%), allocation sequence generation in 19 (95%), and experimenter or handler
0275 blinding in 19 (95%). Random housing or protection against cage/position effects was
0276 unclear in 16 studies (80%) and absent in four (20%).
0277 Some domains were better reported. Outcome assessment followed a fixed or pre-
0278 specified rule in 19 studies (95%), methods and results were fully aligned in 16 (80%),
0279 attrition was adequately handled in 14 (70%), and selective outcome reporting was not
0280 evident in 19 (95%). Animal-welfare compliance was fully reported in 15 studies (75%) and
0281 partly in five (25%). Formal conflict-of-interest statements were present in 12 studies
0282 (60%), and inappropriate funder or commercial influence was judged absent in 13 (65%)
0283 but unclear in seven (35%). Overall, the CRIME-Q assessment identified substantial
0284 uncertainty arising from incomplete reporting of design safeguards, alongside a recurrent
0285 concern about treatment-level replication because acoustic exposure was commonly
0286 shared among multiple animals.
0004 Discussion
0287 
0288 Music exposure reduced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors by about 20% relative to
0289 control conditions across experimental contexts, as indicated by a significantly negative
0290 overall ln𝑅𝑅 (Fig. 3A). This reduction occurred across both anxiety- and depression-like
0291 outcomes (Fig. 4A) and across life stages at exposure (Fig. 5A), indicating that the overall
0292 direction of the association was generally consistent across these broad categories,
0293 despite substantial heterogeneity among effect sizes. However, the magnitude of the
0015 
0294 average response differed among behavioral assays, indicating that the estimated effect of
0295 music also depended on how affect-related behavior was measured. Although effect sizes
0296 showed substantial heterogeneity (Table S4), the predominance of negative effect sizes
0297 supports a clear directional effect. Music exposure did not systematically alter behavioral
0298 variability. The overall lnVR estimate was close to zero and non-significant (Fig. 3B),
0299 showing that music exposure neither consistently reduced nor increased behavioral
0300 dispersion. In other words, music exposure shifted average affect-related behavior without
0301 broadly homogenizing responses or amplifying differences between individuals. This
0302 distinction highlights the conceptual difference between average effects (lnRR), which
0303 capture shifts in average behavior, and variability effects (lnVR), which capture changes in
0304 dispersion (Nakagawa et al. 2015). The absence of a global lnVR effect indicates that
0305 music alters central tendency without uniformly reshaping individual variation. Exploratory
0306 outcome-specific analyses suggested different variability patterns for anxiety- and
0307 depression-like outcomes: variability tended to increase for anxiety-like behaviors and
0308 decrease for depression-like behaviors. However, the depression-specific evidence was
0309 strongly male biased, with female outcomes represented by only six effect sizes from two
0310 studies, so these differences should be interpreted cautiously. Moderator analyses also
0311 revealed potential heterogeneity in variability responses across behavioral assays, music
0312 meta-genres, and experimental designs (Fig. 6B–C). The estimated strain-level variance
0313 component was close to zero in both average and variability models. However, only six
0314 strains were represented in the dataset, and two of these occurred in a single study each.
0315 The near-zero estimate should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that strain has no
0316 biological influence on behavioral responses; rather, the available evidence provides
0317 limited information for estimating strain-level heterogeneity. This is particularly important
0318 because strain differences are known to influence behavioral outcomes (Russo et al. 2012)
0319 4.1  Music exposure in the context of environmental enrichment
0320 The direction of these average effects aligns with findings from other non-pharmacological
0321 interventions, particularly voluntary exercise and environmental enrichment. It is also
0016 
0322 broadly consistent with evidence from human studies reporting reductions in anxiety and
0323 depressive symptoms following music-based interventions (Witte et al. 2022), although the
0324 present results concern experimentally controlled exposure in laboratory rodents and
0325 should not be interpreted as direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy in humans. Exercise
0326 reliably reduces immobility in stress-coping assays and increases exploratory behavior in
0327 anxiety-related tests (Duman et al. 2008; Mul and Mul 2018; Brenes et al. 2020; Yang et al.
0328 2025). Although we cannot directly compare effect magnitudes across paradigms, the
0329 shared direction of effects suggests that music functions as a sensory form of
0330 environmental enrichment rather than as a pharmacological analogue. Music exposure
0331 produced stronger reductions in induced paradigms than in assays measuring baseline
0332 affective behavior (Fig. 5D). Induced models (e.g., chronic stress or learned helplessness)
0333 elevate anxiety- and depression-like phenotypes above typical levels (Mineur et al. 2006),
0334 creating greater scope for behavioral improvement. Interventions that modulate stress
0335 physiology should therefore produce larger effects under heightened disturbance than
0336 under baseline conditions, where responses already fall within a restricted range.
0337 Timing also influenced effect magnitude. Studies that delivered music prior to behavioral
0338 testing showed larger reductions in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors than studies
0339 presenting music concurrently with testing. Rather than contradicting evidence for acute
0340 music-related changes in arousal or cognition (Rickard et al. 2005), this pattern suggests
0341 that music primarily shifts baseline affective states rather than enhancing task
0342 performance. When introduced during testing, music may act as an additional sensory
0343 stimulus that interacts with task demands, whereas prior exposure allows physiological
0344 and affective modulation to occur before assessment. Several mechanisms may account
0345 for these patterns. Music may modulate stress physiology through the hypothalamic–
0346 pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, reducing stress reactivity and downstream glucocorticoid
0347 signaling (Smith and Vale 2006; Mcewen et al. 2007). Alternatively, music may increase
0348 environmental predictability. Chronic unpredictable mild stress reliably induces anxiety-
0349 and depression-like phenotypes (Willner 2017), whereas music exposure typically occurs
0350 in stable, repeatable contexts. By increasing sensory predictability, music may dampen
0017 
0351 stress responses associated with uncertainty. This interpretation aligns with the joint
0352 ln𝑅𝑅–ln𝑉𝑅 pattern: music consistently shifts average behavior while altering variability
0353 only in specific contexts. As with other enrichment-based interventions, heterogeneity is
0354 expected rather than anomalous (Würbel 2001; Simpson and Kelly 2011).
0355 4.2  Assay-, stimulus-, and design-specific variability
0356 Behavioral assays strongly moderated variability responses. Relative to the Elevated Plus
0357 Maze (EPM), music exposure reduced variability in the Forced Swim Test and Tail
0358 Suspension Test (Fig. 6A). These assays constrain animals to a limited behavioral
0359 repertoire and probe stress-coping states (Cryan and Holmes 2005; Leite-Almeida et al.
0360 2022). Under such constraints, music appears to promote convergence toward similar
0361 coping responses, reducing behavioral dispersion across animals. In contrast, music
0362 exposure increased variability in the EPM. The EPM assesses approach–avoidance conflict
0363 and typically produces substantial individual variability (Walf and Frye 2007; Knight et al.
0364 2021; Albani et al. 2015). Music may amplify pre-existing differences in how animals
0365 balance exploration and avoidance, increasing dispersion even when average anxiety-like
0366 behavior declines. Increased variability in this context does not imply adverse effects;
0367 rather, it reflects the sensitivity of this behavioral domain to individual traits and assay
0368 structure.
0369 These findings carry practical implications. Researchers who prioritize reproducibility and
0370 precise estimation of average effects may obtain more consistent outcomes using assays
0371 that exhibit lower variability under music exposure, such as the Forced Swim Test or Tail
0372 Suspension Test. In contrast, assays like the EPM may require larger samples or explicit
0373 modeling of heterogeneity to achieve comparable precision. No assay is inherently
0374 superior; instead, tasks differ in how consistently animals respond under identical auditory
0375 conditions.
0376 Music meta-genre also structured variability. Studies using popular contemporary and
0377 traditional or folk music showed greater dispersion than studies using mixed stimuli,
0018 
0378 whereas Western art or orchestral music did not differ significantly from the reference
0379 category (Fig. 6C). Although genre categories are coarse, they likely capture differences in
0380 auditory temporal structure and predictability. Predictable auditory patterns tend to elicit
0381 more consistent neural responses (Heilbron and Chait 2018; Auksztulewicz et al. 2017),
0382 whereas less predictable rhythms amplify differential processing and expectation
0383 violations (Foldal et al. 2020). Thus, genre-dependent variability likely reflects differences
0384 in how consistently animals encode and integrate auditory input.
0385 Experimental design also influenced variability. Posttest-only designs were associated
0386 with greater behavioral dispersion under music exposure than factorial or repeated-
0387 measures designs (Fig. 6D), as well as more negative average effects relative to factorial
0388 designs. Thus, studies using posttest-only designs tended to report larger reductions in
0389 anxiety- and depression-like behavior, but also a greater music–control difference in
0390 behavioral variability. One possible explanation is that more structured designs can better
0391 isolate music-specific effects from variation associated with baseline states, time, co-
0392 manipulations, or repeated testing. By contrast, posttest-only comparisons may retain
0393 these unmeasured sources of variation within the observed treatment contrast.
0394 Design architecture may therefore affect both the estimated average effect and the
0395 apparent dispersion response, rather than merely acting as a neutral feature of study
0396 implementation. However, this moderator pattern should not be interpreted causally;
0397 design architecture is likely confounded by other experimental features, including the
0398 choice of induced model, exposure protocol, and behavioral assay. Nevertheless, it
0399 indicates that some variability attributed to music exposure may reflect differences in
0400 experimental design rather than genuine biological heterogeneity in individual responses
0401 alone.
0402 4.3  Heterogeneity, reporting practices, and robustness
0403 The combined ln𝑅𝑅 and ln𝑉𝑅 results indicate substantial heterogeneity in how music
0404 exposure was implemented and experienced across studies (Kühlmann et al. 2018; Le et al.
0019 
0405 2025). Differences in genre, duration, timing, acoustic delivery, control conditions, and
0406 experimental context are likely to interact in non-additive ways, complicating mechanistic
0407 inference. Music meta-genre explained little heterogeneity in average effects but
0408 substantial heterogeneity in behavioral variability, indicating that average outcomes alone
0409 can obscure important dimensions of behavioral sensitivity.
0410 The CRIME-Q assessment (Fig. 7) is most informative not as an inventory of missing
0411 information, but as a limit on causal attribution. In an acoustic intervention, the nominal
0412 stimulus does not fully define the treatment received by the animals. Sound level and
0413 frequency spectrum, speaker placement, room acoustics, cage position, background
0414 noise, group housing, and separation from control animals can all modify effective
0415 exposure. Ambient acoustic conditions can vary substantially among rooms and cages,
0416 and differences in acoustic context can alter rodent behavior even when other
0417 experimental procedures are similar (Prior 2006; Barabas et al. 2022). Consequently,
0418 incomplete reporting of exposure delivery and control conditions makes it difficult to
0419 determine whether differences among studies reflect the musical stimulus itself, total
0420 acoustic stimulation, or broader features of the housing environment.
0421 Similarly, unclear ratings for baseline balance, allocation, housing, and blinding do not
0422 demonstrate that the studies were biased; rather, they limit our ability to evaluate the
0423 direction and magnitude of any bias (Hooijmans et al. 2014; Andersen et al. 2024). A more
0424 specific concern emerged for treatment-level replication. Our review of the original
0425 exposure protocols showed that music was usually delivered to several animals sharing a
0426 cage or room, whereas primary analyses generally treated individual animals as
0427 independent replicates. In such designs, the cage or room rather than the individual
0428 animal may constitute the independently replicated treatment unit. Because our sampling
0429 variances were calculated from the animal sample sizes reported by the original studies,
0430 any unaccounted cage- or room-level clustering is inherited by the meta-analysis. Where
0431 the number of animals exceeds the number of independent acoustic exposure units,
0432 sampling variances may therefore be underestimated and individual effect sizes may
0433 receive greater precision than warranted. Cage- and room-level replication was not
0020 
0434 reported consistently enough to reconstruct effective sample sizes retrospectively, so this
0435 limitation could not be corrected quantitatively. Thus, the observed heterogeneity likely
0436 reflects a combination of biological differences, methodological variation, and differences
0437 in experimental structure and precision across studies rather than any single source.
0438 Despite these limitations, multiple diagnostics support the robustness of the overall
0439 average effect: we found no evidence of small-study or time-lag bias, and leave-one-out
0440 analyses showed that no individual study drove the estimated effect. These diagnostics
0441 indicate that the pooled result was not attributable to a single influential study or a clear
0442 publication-related artifact, although they do not address the treatment-level replication
0443 issue described above. Generalization should nevertheless be restricted to the
0444 experimental contexts represented in the literature. Most studies involved young adult
0445 laboratory mice or rats exposed to music over relatively short periods under controlled
0446 housing conditions. The estimated ≈ 20% reduction in anxiety- and depression-like
0447 behaviors should therefore be interpreted as applying primarily to comparable laboratory
0448 settings. Extrapolation to other developmental stages, longer exposures, other species, or
0449 more complex environments requires direct testing.
0450 Other design features also remain insufficiently characterized. Reporting of the light–dark
0451 phase during music exposure or behavioral testing was uncommon, limiting assessment of
0452 whether circadian timing contributed to variation in either average effects or behavioral
0453 variability (Saré et al. 2021). In addition, most primary studies were designed to detect
0454 average differences rather than changes in behavioral variability. The context-dependent
0455 ln𝑉𝑅 patterns should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the smaller
0456 evidence base and exploratory nature of the moderator analyses.
0457 4.4  Study-specific limitations and recommendations for future
0458 research
0459 The CRIME-Q assessment identifies several priorities for future research on music
0460 exposure and affect-related behavior. Studies should report music and control conditions
0021 
0461 in sufficient detail to permit replication, including the stimulus identity or composition,
0462 delivery hardware, sound level, speaker–cage arrangement, exposure schedule, acoustic
0463 separation between treatment and control animals, and whether exposure was delivered
0464 at the cage, room, or animal level. Reporting the light–dark phase of exposure and testing,
0465 habituation procedures, housing conditions, and cage or room allocation would further
0466 clarify potential circadian and environmental sources of variation. Future experiments
0467 should also strengthen safeguards against bias by reporting a priori sample-size
0468 calculations, explicit allocation methods, baseline group characteristics, housing or
0469 position randomization, blinding of handlers and outcome assessors where feasible, and
0470 animal-level attrition and exclusions by treatment group. Prespecifying outcomes and
0471 making protocols or analysis plans available would improve the assessment of selective
0472 reporting. Finally, studies should be designed to assess both average responses and
0473 behavioral variability. This will require larger samples, repeated-measures designs where
0474 appropriate, and reporting of individual-level data or response distributions rather than
0475 group summaries alone. The experimental unit should be specified explicitly, with cage- or
0476 room-level replication and hierarchical analyses used when exposure or housing is shared
0477 among animals. Together, these improvements would allow future work to distinguish
0478 more clearly between music-specific effects, broader acoustic or housing effects, and
0479 genuine differences in individual behavioral responsiveness.
0480 4.5  Conclusions
0481 This meta-analysis shows that music exposure is associated, on average, with an
0482 approximately 20% reduction in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in laboratory
0483 rodents, while effects on behavioral variability are strongly context dependent. Music
0484 exposure primarily shifts the central tendency of affect-related behavior, with changes in
0485 behavioral dispersion emerging only under specific experimental and stimulus conditions.
0486 Joint consideration of average effects and variability therefore provides a more complete
0487 framework for interpreting heterogeneous experimental results and for evaluating sensory
0488 and environmental interventions.

0559 5.4  Deviations from the preregistered protocol
0560 We implemented six refinements to our protocol after preregistration. To accommodate
0561 heterogeneity in reporting practices, we introduced the first three refinements during data
0562 extraction, whereas the final two were added as post hoc assessments. Crucially, these
0563 adjustments did not alter the PECO framework, eligibility criteria, outcome definitions, or
0564 primary analytical strategy.
0565 First, to model statistical dependence among effect sizes, we coded the comparison
0566 structure of each effect size as either independent or dependent (within-subject/repeated-
0567 measures). This expansion of our preregistered metadata structure allowed for accurate
0568 sampling-variance specification when a single experimental cohort yielded multiple effect
0569 sizes.
0025 
0570 Second, to harmonize heterogeneous reporting, we classified exposure durations (acute,
0571 short-term, medium-term, or long-term) and grouped music stimuli into broad meta-
0572 genres based on historical context and dominant musical features (see Table 1 for
0573 definitions). We derived these classifications from the reported text to facilitate
0574 interpretable moderator analyses.
0575 Third, we recorded whether studies explicitly reported key stimulus parameters, such as
0576 loudness, frequency, and light–dark phases. Because loudness and frequency were
0577 frequently under-reported, we explicitly coded instances of non-reporting instead of
0578 treating them as missing data, allowing us to quantify reporting gaps.
0579 Fourth, we introduced a post hoc critical appraisal of the included evidence using the
0580 Critical Appraisal of Methodological (technical) Quality, Quality of Reporting and Risk of
0581 Bias in Animal Research (CRIME-Q) framework (Andersen et al. 2024). We evaluated each
0582 eligible study against 20 item-level criteria spanning quality of reporting, methodological
0583 quality, and risk of bias.
0584 Fifth, following concerns about the level of treatment replication, we conducted a post hoc
0585 targeted review of the original exposure protocols to determine whether music was
0586 delivered at the animal, cage, or room level and whether the primary analyses accounted
0587 for shared exposure or housing structure. This assessment was used to evaluate the
0588 potential for pseudoreplication and overestimation of precision arising when multiple
0589 animals shared the same acoustic exposure but were analyzed as independent
0590 observations.
0591 Sixth, we conducted an additional post hoc sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness
0592 of the results to the correlation assumptions used to model non-independence. We varied
0593 both the within-subject correlation used for dependent comparisons (𝑟) and the
0594 correlation among sampling errors of multiple effect sizes from the same cohort (𝜌) across
0595 values of 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 0.90, both separately and jointly. We then examined
0596 whether the overall ln𝑅𝑅 and ln𝑉𝑅 estimates, and the anxiety–depression contrast in ln𝑉𝑅,
0597 changed across these assumptions.

0671 5.6  Meta-analytic models
0672 We fitted multilevel meta-analytic models separately for lnRR and lnVR using restricted
0673 maximum likelihood (REML) estimation in metafor (Viechtbauer 2010). Because individual
0674 studies often contributed multiple, non-independent effect sizes (e.g., multiple outcomes,
0675 assays, or contrasts from the same experimental cohort), we explicitly modeled
0676 dependence among effect sizes in two ways. First, sampling covariances were
0677 incorporated using a variance–covariance matrix (𝑽) constructed at the cohort level, such
0678 that effect sizes derived from the same cohort were assumed to have correlated sampling
0679 errors (with 𝜌 = 0.5 when within-cohort correlations were not reported), yielding a block-
0680 diagonal-structured 𝑽 matrix (Noble et al. 2017; Nakagawa et al. 2022). Second, we
0681 included random intercepts for study ID to account for between-study heterogeneity, for
0682 effect size ID to accommodate residual within-study variation among effect sizes beyond
0683 sampling error, and for strain to control for systematic differences among rodent strains.
0684 Moderator analyses were conducted as separate uni-moderator meta-regressions using
0685 the same random-effects structure and the same 𝑽 matrix as the corresponding overall
0686 model. Given the number of moderators and pairwise contrasts relative to the size of the
0687 evidence base, these analyses were treated as exploratory. We did not apply a formal
0688 multiple-comparison correction because stringent family-wise error corrections can
0689 substantially reduce statistical power and increase the risk of false-negative results when
0690 many related hypotheses are evaluated (Pike 2011; Drezner and Drezner 2016, Gelman et
0691 al. 2012). Instead, we considered the magnitude and uncertainty of estimated effects
0692 together with the proportion of heterogeneity explained by each moderator, and
0693 interpreted individual contrasts as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.
0694 Inference was based on 𝑡-tests as implemented in metafor, and all models were fitted
0695 using REML.
