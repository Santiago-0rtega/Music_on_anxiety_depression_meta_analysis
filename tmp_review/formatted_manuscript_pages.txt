          Abstract                                                                  
      20                                                                            
                                                                                    
      21  Music exposure may influence affect-related behavior in rodents, but results vary across studies, and effects
      22  on behavioral variability remain unclear. We conducted a preregistered systematic review and multilevel
                                                                                    
      23  meta-analysis of 20 experimental studies (298 effect sizes) testing music exposure in laboratory rodents.
      24  Using the log response ratio (lnRR) and log variability ratio (lnVR), we estimated changes in average anxiety-
      25  and depression-like behavior and behavioral dispersion while accounting for non-independence among
                                                                                    
      26  effects. We also assessed reporting, methodological quality, and risk of bias using CRIME-Q. Music exposure
      27  was associated with an approximately 20% reduction in anxiety- and depression-like behavior relative to
                                                                                    
      28  control conditions, with no overall change in behavioral variability. Exploratory moderator analyses
      29  suggested context-dependent dispersion: anxiety-like outcomes tended toward greater variability and
      30  depression-like outcomes toward lower variability, while behavioral assay and music meta-genre explained
                                                                                    
      31  comparatively more heterogeneity in lnVR. Reporting of sample-size justification, allocation, blinding,
      32  attrition, and acoustic delivery was often incomplete. The average reduction applies primarily to the studied
                                                                                    
      33  laboratory contexts; variability effects and generalization beyond predominantly young adult rodents and
      34  short-term exposure require caution.                                      
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
      35  Keywords— anxiety; depression; rodent models; auditory enrichment; behavioral
                                                                                    
      36  variability; multilevel meta-analysis

          2    Introduction                                                         
      41                                                                            
                                                                                    
      42  Anxiety and depressive disorders are among the most prevalent and disabling psychiatric
                                                                                    
      43  conditions worldwide (Ferrari, 2022; Spytska, 2024). Anxiety disorders are characterized by
                                                                                    
      44  persistent fear, hypervigilance, and heightened stress reactivity, whereas depressive
      45  disorders involve sustained low mood, anhedonia, and impaired motivation. Together,
                                                                                    
      46  these conditions affect hundreds of millions of people globally, causing a substantial
                                                                                    
      47  economic burden and reducing quality of life (Ferrari, 2022; Spytska, 2024; Ridley et al.,
      48  2020). Their high prevalence and chronic nature have driven extensive efforts to
                                                                                    
      49  understand the neural mechanisms underlying affective dysregulation and to develop
                                                                                    
      50  effective treatments. Pharmacological therapies remain a cornerstone of treatment, but
      51  their efficacy varies and they are often accompanied by side effects or incomplete
                                                                                    
      52  symptom relief (Boulenger, 2004; Kelly and Mezuk, 2017). Consequently, interest has
                                                                                    
      53  grown in complementary, non-pharmacological approaches that modulate affective states
                                                                                    
      54  by engaging neural systems involved in emotion regulation and stress responsiveness.
      55  Among these, music exposure has received substantial attention (Witte et al., 2022). In
                                                                                    
      56  humans, music reliably engages limbic, paralimbic, and cortical networks implicated in
                                                                                    
      57  emotion, reward, and stress regulation, eliciting robust physiological and affective
      58  responses (Arnold et al., 2024; Koelsch, 2014; Levitin and Tirovolas, 2009). Clinical and
                                                                                    
      59  experimental studies suggest that music can reduce self-reported anxiety and depressive
                                                                                    
      60  symptoms, at least transiently, and may enhance emotional regulation across diverse
      61  contexts (Fancourt and Finn, 2019; Thoma et al. 2013).                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
      62  Although the neural mechanisms through which music engages affective and stress-
      63  related systems are increasingly well characterized, translating these findings into reliable
                                                                                    
      64  therapeutic applications remains challenging (Koelsch, 2014; Fancourt et al., 2014).
                                                                                    
      65  Individual differences in musical preference, prior experience, clinical condition, and
                                                                                    
      66  cultural context introduce substantial variability in outcomes. This variability complicates
      67  assessments of when music exposure can consistently modulate anxiety- and depression-
                                                                                    
      68  related processes (Balkwill and Thompson, 1999; McDermott et al., 2016). Preclinical
                                                                                    
          3

      69  animal models provide a valuable framework to address these limitations. Rodent models
      70  allow precise control over auditory exposure, developmental timing, and environmental
                                                                                    
      71  conditions, while enabling standardized assessments of anxiety- and depression-like
                                                                                    
      72  behaviors. Behavioral assays such as the Elevated Plus Maze, Open Field Test, Light–Dark
                                                                                    
      73  Box, Forced Swim Test, Tail Suspension Test, and Sucrose Preference Test are widely used
      74  to probe stress reactivity, avoidance behavior, and coping strategies under controlled
                                                                                    
      75  conditions (e.g., Cryan and Holmes, 2005; Slattery and Cryan, 2012). These models permit
                                                                                    
      76  systematic investigation of how environmental inputs interact with neural and endocrine
      77  systems implicated in affective regulation, including stress-related and motivational
                                                                                    
      78  pathways (Mcewen et al., 2007; Nestler et al., 2002).                     
                                                                                    
      79  Within this experimental framework, a growing literature has examined how music
                                                                                    
      80  exposure affects rodent behavior. Many studies report reductions in anxiety- or
                                                                                    
      81  depression-like behaviors following music exposure, consistent with the idea that
      82  temporally patterned auditory stimuli such as music can modulate stress-related
                                                                                    
      83  processes (e.g., Escribano et al., 2014; Chikahisa et al., 2007). However, studies differ in
                                                                                    
      84  the type of music used, exposure duration and timing, developmental stage at exposure,
      85  behavioral assays employed, control conditions, and whether behaviors are assessed
                                                                                    
      86  under baseline conditions or following experimentally induced stress (Kühlmann et al.,
                                                                                    
      87  2018; Le et al., 2025). Consequently, reported effects vary widely, leaving it unclear
                                                                                    
      88  whether music exposure exerts a generalizable effect on affect-related behavior or
      89  whether its influence is highly context-dependent. A further limitation of the existing
                                                                                    
      90  literature is its near-exclusive focus on changes in average behavioral responses. Yet
                                                                                    
      91  behavioral variability is itself a biologically meaningful property (Westneat et al., 2015).
      92  Changes in variance can reflect altered stability, sensitivity to environmental perturbations,
                                                                                    
      93  or heterogeneity in individual stress responsiveness. Environmental manipulations may
                                                                                    
      94  reduce variability by stabilizing behavioral responses or increase variability by amplifying
      95  individual differences (Sih et al., 2015). Experimental evidence also shows that
                                                                                    
      96  pharmacological exposures can alter behavioral variability itself: psychoactive
                                                                                    
      97  compounds have been shown to suppress differences among individuals in fish behavior,
                                                                                    
          4                                                                         
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
### PAGE 5
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
      98  including across different social contexts (Polverino et al., 2021; Rafeeq et al., 2026).
      99  Whether music exposure similarly affects behavioral variability alongside average behavior
                                                                                    
     100  has not been systematically examined.                                     
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     101  Recent advances in meta-analytic methodology allow a more rigorous synthesis of this
     102  evidence. Multilevel meta-analytic models accommodate multiple, non-independent
                                                                                    
     103  effect sizes from the same study while appropriately partitioning sources of variance
                                                                                    
     104  (Nakagawa and Santos, 2012). Effect size metrics such as the log response ratio (ln𝑅𝑅)
     105  enable comparison across diverse behavioral measures, whereas variance-based metrics
                                                                                    
     106  such as the log variability ratio (ln𝑉𝑅) permit explicit evaluation of changes in behavioral
                                                                                    
     107  variability (Nakagawa et al., 2015; Senior et al., 2020). Together, these approaches allow
     108  researchers to assess both the average effects of music exposure on affect-related
                                                                                    
     109  behavior and the consistency of those effects across individuals and experimental
                                                                                    
     110  contexts.                                                                 
                                                                                    
     111  Here, we present a preregistered systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis of
                                                                                    
     112  experimental studies examining how music exposure affects anxiety- and depression-like
                                                                                    
     113  behaviors in laboratory rodents. We focused on laboratory mice and rats experimentally
     114  exposed to music and compared with concurrent non-music controls, with eligibility
                                                                                    
     115  restricted to studies reporting standardized anxiety- or depression-like behavioral
                                                                                    
     116  outcomes. Reported in accordance with PRISMA and PRISMA-EcoEvo guidelines (O’Dea et
     117  al., 2021; Page et al., 2021), this synthesis quantifies both average behavioral effects and
                                                                                    
     118  changes in behavioral variability, identifies sources of heterogeneity, and evaluates the
                                                                                    
     119  robustness of reported effects. By clarifying the magnitude, consistency, and context
     120  dependence of music-induced behavioral changes under controlled conditions, this study
                                                                                    
     121  provides a quantitative and conceptual framework for interpreting preclinical evidence
                                                                                    
     122  relevant to affective regulation and hypotheses about sensory modulation.

     124  3.1  Data overview                                                        
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     125  Twenty studies met the predefined inclusion criteria (Flores-Gutiérrez et al., 2018; Fu et al.,
                                                                                    
     126  2025, 2023; Sampaio et al., 2017; Chikahisa et al., 2007; Papadakakis et al., 2019;
                                                                                    
     127  Escribano et al., 2014; Saghari et al., 2021; Krishnamurthy and Rao, 2025; Camargo et al.,
     128  2013; Li et al., 2010; Rizzolo et al., 2021; Uşak et al. 2020; Freitas Oliveira et al., 2020;
                                                                                    
     129  Cheng et al., 2024; Milbratz de Camargo et al., 2017; Ren and Lu, 2024; Chen et al., 2019;
                                                                                    
     130  Pangemanan et al., 2024; Niehues da Cruz et al., 2011), yielding 298 effect sizes for meta-
     131  analysis. Study selection and exclusion decisions are summarized in the PRISMA flow
                                                                                    
     132  diagram (Fig. 1). Reported sample sizes were generally modest, averaging 𝑛 = 9.76
                                                                                    
     133  animals in control conditions (SD = 2.53; median = 10) and 𝑛 = 9.81 animals in music-
     134  exposed conditions (SD = 2.59; median = 10), with study-level average sample sizes
                                                                                    
     135  ranging from 6 to 14 animals per condition. These values represent the numbers of animals
                                                                                    
     136  contributing to the reported outcomes rather than necessarily the number of
     137  independently replicated exposure units. Most effect sizes were derived from anxiety-like
                                                                                    
     138  outcomes (77%, 𝑛 = 231), followed by depression-like outcomes (𝑛 = 64), with three
                                                                                    
     139  effect sizes classified as representing both outcome types. Effect sizes were nearly evenly
                                                                                    
     140  split between mice (Mus musculus, 48%, four strains) and rats (Rattus norvegicus, 52%,
     141  two strains; Fig. 2A), with all analyzed comparisons derived from wild-type or otherwise
                                                                                    
     142  non-genetically modified animals. Tested animals were predominantly male (60%),
                                                                                    
     143  followed by female (38%) and mixed-sex cohorts (2%). This imbalance was especially
     144  pronounced for depression-like outcomes: only 6 of 64 effect sizes (9.4%) were derived
                                                                                    
     145  from females, representing just two independent studies. The age distribution was strongly
                                                                                    
     146  skewed toward young adults (61%), with relatively few effect sizes derived from juvenile or
     147  older animals. Music exposure was most often acute (≤ 7 days; 50%), and the Elevated
                                                                                    
     148  Plus Maze was the most frequently used behavioral assay, followed by the Open Field Test
                                                                                    
     149  (Fig. 2B). Only approximately 18% of effect sizes were derived from animals in which

          4    Discussion                                                           
     294                                                                            
                                                                                    
     295  Music exposure reduced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors by about 20% relative to
                                                                                    
     296  control conditions across experimental contexts, as indicated by a significantly negative
                                                                                    
     297  overall ln𝑅𝑅 (Fig. 3A). This reduction occurred across both anxiety- and depression-like
     298  outcomes (Fig. 4A) and across life stages at exposure (Fig. 5A), indicating that the overall
                                                                                    
     299  direction of the association was generally consistent across these broad categories,
                                                                                    
     300  despite substantial heterogeneity among effect sizes. However, the magnitude of the
                                                                                    
          15                                                                        
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
### PAGE 16
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     301  average response differed among behavioral assays, indicating that the estimated effect of
     302  music also depended on how affect-related behavior was measured. Although effect sizes
                                                                                    
     303  showed substantial heterogeneity (Table S4), the predominance of negative effect sizes is
                                                                                    
     304  consistent with an overall directional association. Music exposure did not systematically
                                                                                    
     305  alter behavioral variability. The overall lnVR estimate was close to zero and non-significant
     306  (Fig. 3B), showing that music exposure neither consistently reduced nor increased
                                                                                    
     307  behavioral dispersion. In other words, music exposure shifted average affect-related
                                                                                    
     308  behavior without broadly homogenizing responses or amplifying differences between
     309  individuals. This distinction highlights the conceptual difference between average effects
                                                                                    
     310  (lnRR), which capture shifts in average behavior, and variability effects (lnVR), which
                                                                                    
     311  capture changes in dispersion (Nakagawa et al., 2015). The absence of a global lnVR effect
     312  indicates that music exposure was associated with a shift in central tendency without a
                                                                                    
     313  consistent change in individual variation. Exploratory outcome-specific analyses
                                                                                    
     314  suggested different variability patterns for anxiety- and depression-like outcomes:
     315  variability tended to increase for anxiety-like behaviors and decrease for depression-like
                                                                                    
     316  behaviors. However, the depression-specific evidence was strongly male biased, with
                                                                                    
     317  female outcomes represented by only six effect sizes from two studies, so these
                                                                                    
     318  differences should be interpreted cautiously. Moderator analyses also revealed potential
     319  heterogeneity in variability responses across behavioral assays, music meta-genres, and
                                                                                    
     320  experimental designs (Fig. 6B–C). The estimated strain-level variance component was
                                                                                    
     321  close to zero in both average and variability models. However, only six strains were
     322  represented in the dataset, and two of these occurred in a single study each. The near-zero
                                                                                    
     323  estimate should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that strain has no biological
                                                                                    
     324  influence on behavioral responses; rather, the available evidence provides limited
     325  information for estimating strain-level heterogeneity. This is particularly important
                                                                                    
     326  because strain differences are known to influence behavioral outcomes (Russo et al., 2012)

     327  4.1  Music exposure in the context of environmental enrichment            
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     328  The direction of these average effects aligns with findings from other non-pharmacological
     329  interventions, particularly voluntary exercise and environmental enrichment. It is also
                                                                                    
     330  broadly consistent with evidence from human studies reporting reductions in anxiety and
                                                                                    
     331  depressive symptoms following music-based interventions (Witte et al., 2022), although
     332  the present results concern experimentally controlled exposure in laboratory rodents and
                                                                                    
     333  should not be interpreted as direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy in humans. Exercise
                                                                                    
     334  reliably reduces immobility in stress-coping assays and increases exploratory behavior in
                                                                                    
     335  anxiety-related tests (Duman et al., 2008; Mul and Mul, 2018; Brenes et al., 2020; Yang et
     336  al., 2025). Although we cannot directly compare effect magnitudes across paradigms, the
                                                                                    
     337  shared direction of effects suggests that music may act as a sensory form of
                                                                                    
     338  environmental enrichment rather than as a pharmacological analogue. Music exposure
     339  produced stronger reductions in induced paradigms than in assays measuring baseline
                                                                                    
     340  affective behavior (Fig. 5D). Induced models (e.g., chronic stress or learned helplessness)
                                                                                    
     341  elevate anxiety- and depression-like phenotypes above typical levels (Mineur et al., 2006),
     342  creating greater scope for behavioral improvement. Interventions that modulate stress
                                                                                    
     343  physiology could therefore show larger effects under heightened disturbance than under
                                                                                    
     344  baseline conditions, where responses already fall within a restricted range.
                                                                                    
     345  Exposure timing was also associated with effect magnitude. Studies that delivered music
                                                                                    
     346  prior to behavioral testing showed larger reductions in anxiety- and depression-like
     347  behaviors than studies presenting music concurrently with testing. Rather than
                                                                                    
     348  contradicting evidence for acute music-related changes in arousal or cognition (Rickard et
                                                                                    
     349  al., 2005), this pattern is consistent with a possible influence on pretest affective state,
                                                                                    
     350  although differences in exposure protocols or study design could also contribute. When
     351  introduced during testing, music may act as an additional sensory stimulus that interacts
                                                                                    
     352  with task demands, whereas prior exposure may permit physiological and affective
                                                                                    
     353  modulation before assessment. Several mechanisms may account for these patterns.
     354  Music may modulate stress physiology through the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA)
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
          17                                                                        
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
### PAGE 18
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     355  axis, reducing stress reactivity and downstream glucocorticoid signaling (Smith and Vale,
     356  2006; Mcewen et al., 2007). Alternatively, music may increase environmental predictability.
                                                                                    
     357  Chronic unpredictable mild stress reliably induces anxiety- and depression-like
                                                                                    
     358  phenotypes (Willner, 2017), whereas music exposure typically occurs in stable, repeatable
                                                                                    
     359  contexts. By increasing sensory predictability, music may dampen stress responses
     360  associated with uncertainty. This interpretation aligns with the joint ln𝑅𝑅–ln𝑉𝑅 pattern:
                                                                                    
     361  music consistently shifts average behavior while altering variability only in specific
                                                                                    
     362  contexts. As with other enrichment-based interventions, heterogeneity is expected rather
     363  than anomalous (Würbel, 2001; Simpson and Kelly, 2011).                   
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     364  4.2  Assay-, stimulus-, and design-specific variability                   
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     365  Behavioral assays were associated with marked differences in variability responses.
     366  Relative to the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM), music exposure reduced variability in the Forced
                                                                                    
     367  Swim Test and Tail Suspension Test (Fig. 6A). These assays constrain animals to a limited
                                                                                    
     368  behavioral repertoire and probe stress-coping states (Cryan and Holmes, 2005; Leite-
     369  Almeida et al., 2022). Under such constraints, the observed pattern is consistent with
                                                                                    
     370  more similar coping responses, reducing behavioral dispersion across animals. In contrast,
                                                                                    
     371  music exposure increased variability in the EPM. The EPM assesses approach–avoidance
     372  conflict and typically produces substantial individual variability (Walf and Frye, 2007;
                                                                                    
     373  Knight et al., 2021; Albani et al., 2015). This pattern could reflect pre-existing differences in
                                                                                    
     374  how animals balance exploration and avoidance, increasing dispersion even when average
                                                                                    
     375  anxiety-like behavior declines. Increased variability in this context does not imply adverse
     376  effects; rather, it reflects the sensitivity of this behavioral domain to individual traits and
                                                                                    
     377  assay structure.                                                          
                                                                                    
     378  These findings carry practical implications. Researchers who prioritize reproducibility and
                                                                                    
     379  precise estimation of average effects may obtain more consistent outcomes using assays
                                                                                    
     380  that exhibit lower variability under music exposure, such as the Forced Swim Test or Tail
     381  Suspension Test. In contrast, assays like the EPM may require larger samples or explicit
                                                                                    
     382  modeling of heterogeneity to achieve comparable precision. No assay is inherently
                                                                                    
          18                                                                        
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
### PAGE 19
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     383  superior; instead, tasks differ in how consistently animals respond under identical auditory
     384  conditions.                                                               
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     385  Music meta-genre was also associated with differences in variability. Studies using
                                                                                    
     386  popular contemporary and traditional or folk music showed greater dispersion than
     387  studies using mixed stimuli, whereas Western art or orchestral music did not differ
                                                                                    
     388  significantly from the reference category (Fig. 6C). Although genre categories are coarse,
                                                                                    
     389  they may partly reflect differences in auditory temporal structure and predictability.
     390  Predictable auditory patterns tend to elicit more consistent neural responses (Heilbron
                                                                                    
     391  and Chait, 2018; Auksztulewicz et al., 2017), whereas less predictable rhythms amplify
                                                                                    
     392  differential processing and expectation violations (Foldal et al., 2020). Thus, genre-
     393  associated variability could reflect differences in how consistently animals encode and
                                                                                    
     394  integrate auditory input.                                                 
                                                                                    
     395  Experimental design was also associated with variability. Posttest-only designs were
                                                                                    
     396  associated with greater behavioral dispersion under music exposure than factorial or
                                                                                    
     397  repeated-measures designs (Fig. 6D), as well as more negative average effects relative to
     398  factorial designs. Thus, studies using posttest-only designs tended to report larger
                                                                                    
     399  reductions in anxiety- and depression-like behavior, but also a greater music–control
                                                                                    
     400  difference in behavioral variability. One possible explanation is that more structured
                                                                                    
     401  designs can better isolate music-specific effects from variation associated with baseline
     402  states, time, co-manipulations, or repeated testing. By contrast, posttest-only
                                                                                    
     403  comparisons may retain these unmeasured sources of variation within the observed
                                                                                    
     404  treatment contrast.                                                       
                                                                                    
     405  Design architecture may therefore affect both the estimated average effect and the
                                                                                    
     406  apparent dispersion response, rather than merely acting as a neutral feature of study
     407  implementation. However, this moderator pattern should not be interpreted causally;
                                                                                    
     408  design architecture is likely confounded by other experimental features, including the
                                                                                    
     409  choice of induced model, exposure protocol, and behavioral assay. Nevertheless, it
     410  indicates that some variability attributed to music exposure may reflect differences in

     411  experimental design rather than genuine biological heterogeneity in individual responses
     412  alone.                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     413  4.3  Heterogeneity, reporting practices, and robustness                   
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     414  The combined ln𝑅𝑅 and ln𝑉𝑅 results indicate substantial heterogeneity in how music
     415  exposure was implemented and experienced across studies (Kühlmann et al., 2018; Le et
                                                                                    
     416  al., 2025). Differences in genre, duration, timing, acoustic delivery, control conditions, and
                                                                                    
     417  experimental context are likely to interact in non-additive ways, complicating mechanistic
                                                                                    
     418  inference. Music meta-genre explained little heterogeneity in average effects but
     419  substantial heterogeneity in behavioral variability, indicating that average outcomes alone
                                                                                    
     420  can obscure important dimensions of behavioral sensitivity.               
                                                                                    
     421  The CRIME-Q assessment (Fig. 7) is most informative not as an inventory of missing
                                                                                    
     422  information, but as a limit on causal attribution. In an acoustic intervention, the nominal
                                                                                    
     423  stimulus does not fully define the treatment received by the animals. Sound level and
     424  frequency spectrum, speaker placement, room acoustics, cage position, background
                                                                                    
     425  noise, group housing, and separation from control animals can all modify effective
                                                                                    
     426  exposure. Ambient acoustic conditions can vary substantially among rooms and cages,
     427  and differences in acoustic context can alter rodent behavior even when other
                                                                                    
     428  experimental procedures are similar (Prior, 2006; Barabas et al., 2022). Consequently,
                                                                                    
     429  incomplete reporting of exposure delivery and control conditions makes it difficult to
                                                                                    
     430  determine whether differences among studies reflect the musical stimulus itself, total
     431  acoustic stimulation, or broader features of the housing environment.     
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     432  Similarly, unclear ratings for baseline balance, allocation, housing, and blinding do not
     433  demonstrate that the studies were biased; rather, they limit our ability to evaluate the
                                                                                    
     434  direction and magnitude of any bias (Hooijmans et al., 2014; Andersen et al., 2024). A more
                                                                                    
     435  specific concern emerged for treatment-level replication. Our review of the original
     436  exposure protocols showed that music was usually delivered to several animals sharing a
                                                                                    
     437  cage or room, whereas primary analyses generally treated individual animals as
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
          20                                                                        
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
### PAGE 21
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     438  independent replicates. In such designs, the cage or room rather than the individual
     439  animal may constitute the independently replicated treatment unit. Because our sampling
                                                                                    
     440  variances were calculated from the animal sample sizes reported by the original studies,
                                                                                    
     441  any unaccounted cage- or room-level clustering is inherited by the meta-analysis. Where
                                                                                    
     442  the number of animals exceeds the number of independent acoustic exposure units,
     443  sampling variances may therefore be underestimated and individual effect sizes may
                                                                                    
     444  receive greater precision than warranted. Cage- and room-level replication was not
                                                                                    
     445  reported consistently enough to reconstruct effective sample sizes retrospectively, so this
     446  limitation could not be corrected quantitatively. Thus, the observed heterogeneity likely
                                                                                    
     447  reflects a combination of biological differences, methodological variation, and differences
                                                                                    
     448  in experimental structure and precision across studies rather than any single source.
                                                                                    
     449  Despite these limitations, multiple diagnostics support the robustness of the overall
                                                                                    
     450  average effect: we found no evidence of small-study or time-lag bias, and leave-one-out
     451  analyses showed that no individual study drove the estimated effect. These diagnostics
                                                                                    
     452  indicate that the pooled result was not attributable to a single influential study or a clear
                                                                                    
     453  publication-related artifact, although they do not address the treatment-level replication
     454  issue described above. Generalization should nevertheless be restricted to the
                                                                                    
     455  experimental contexts represented in the literature. Most studies involved young adult
                                                                                    
     456  laboratory mice or rats exposed to music over relatively short periods under controlled
                                                                                    
     457  housing conditions. The estimated ≈ 20% reduction in anxiety- and depression-like
     458  behaviors should therefore be interpreted as applying primarily to comparable laboratory
                                                                                    
     459  settings. Extrapolation to other developmental stages, longer exposures, other species, or
                                                                                    
     460  more complex environments requires direct testing.                        
                                                                                    
     461  Other design features also remain insufficiently characterized. Reporting of the light–dark
                                                                                    
     462  phase during music exposure or behavioral testing was uncommon, limiting assessment of
     463  whether circadian timing contributed to variation in either average effects or behavioral
                                                                                    
     464  variability (Saré et al., 2021). In addition, most primary studies were designed to detect
                                                                                    
     465  average differences rather than changes in behavioral variability. The context-dependent

### PAGE 22
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     466  ln𝑉𝑅 patterns should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the smaller
     467  evidence base and exploratory nature of the moderator analyses.           
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     468  4.4  Study-specific limitations and recommendations for future            
                                                                                    
     469  research                                                                  
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     470  The CRIME-Q assessment identifies several priorities for future research on music
                                                                                    
     471  exposure and affect-related behavior. Studies should report music and control conditions
     472  in sufficient detail to permit replication, including the stimulus identity or composition,
                                                                                    
     473  delivery hardware, sound level, speaker–cage arrangement, exposure schedule, acoustic
                                                                                    
     474  separation between treatment and control animals, and whether exposure was delivered
     475  at the cage, room, or animal level. Reporting the light–dark phase of exposure and testing,
                                                                                    
     476  habituation procedures, housing conditions, and cage or room allocation would further
                                                                                    
     477  clarify potential circadian and environmental sources of variation. Future experiments
                                                                                    
     478  should also strengthen safeguards against bias by reporting a priori sample-size
     479  calculations, explicit allocation methods, baseline group characteristics, housing or
                                                                                    
     480  position randomization, blinding of handlers and outcome assessors where feasible, and
                                                                                    
     481  animal-level attrition and exclusions by treatment group. Prespecifying outcomes and
     482  making protocols or analysis plans available would improve the assessment of selective
                                                                                    
     483  reporting. Finally, studies should be designed to assess both average responses and
                                                                                    
     484  behavioral variability. This will require larger samples, repeated-measures designs where
     485  appropriate, and reporting of individual-level data or response distributions rather than
                                                                                    
     486  group summaries alone. The experimental unit should be specified explicitly, with cage- or
                                                                                    
     487  room-level replication and hierarchical analyses used when exposure or housing is shared
     488  among animals. Together, these improvements would allow future work to distinguish
                                                                                    
     489  more clearly between music-specific effects, broader acoustic or housing effects, and
                                                                                    
     490  genuine differences in individual behavioral responsiveness.

     491  4.5  Conclusions                                                          
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     492  This meta-analysis shows that music exposure is associated, on average, with an
     493  approximately 20% reduction in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in laboratory
                                                                                    
     494  rodents, while effects on behavioral variability are strongly context dependent. The
                                                                                    
     495  available evidence more consistently supports a shift in average affect-related behavior,
     496  while differences in behavioral dispersion appeared in some experimental and stimulus
                                                                                    
     497  contexts. Joint consideration of average effects and variability therefore provides a more
                                                                                    
     498  complete framework for interpreting heterogeneous experimental results and for
                                                                                    
     499  evaluating sensory and environmental interventions.

     520  5.2  Eligibility criteria and screening                                   
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     521  Study eligibility criteria followed the preregistered protocol and PECO (Population,
                                                                                    
     522  Exposure, Comparator, Outcome; Table S5) framework. In brief, to be eligible for inclusion,
                                                                                    
     523  studies had to report experiments assessing the effects of music exposure on anxiety- or
     524  depression-like behaviors in laboratory mice or rats. We excluded studies using wild-
                                                                                    
     525  caught animals. For genetically modified models or animals subjected to invasive
                                                                                    
     526  interventions (e.g., surgical or pharmacological procedures), we only included data from
     527  wild-type or sham-operated groups, if available; valid comparisons were restricted to
                                                                                    
     528  music-exposed versus control animals within the same genetic or procedural background.
                                                                                    
     529  Studies relying exclusively on physiological or histological outcomes without standardized
     530  behavioral assays were excluded. We also excluded studies lacking a concurrent control
                                                                                    
     531  group because treatment effects could not be estimated without a comparator. We used
                                                                                    
     532  Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016) for screening titles and abstracts from bibliographic records.
     533  English-language records were screened independently by at least two reviewers (S.O.,
                                                                                    
     534  M.L., A.L., A.M., E.L., S.N., and S.P.), whereas non-English records were screened by a
                                                                                    
     535  single reviewer with relevant language expertise. Full-text screening followed the same
                                                                                    
     536  procedure, also in Rayyan. Screening of non-English language records and studies was
     537  conducted by reviewers with relevant language expertise (e.g., S.P., A.L., and S.O. for
                                                                                    
     538  Spanish; M.L. for Polish and Russian; A.M. for Japanese).

     570  5.4  Deviations from the preregistered protocol                           
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     571  We implemented six refinements to our protocol after preregistration. To accommodate
     572  heterogeneity in reporting practices, we introduced the first three refinements during data
                                                                                    
     573  extraction, whereas the final two were added as post hoc assessments. Crucially, these
                                                                                    
     574  adjustments did not alter the PECO framework, eligibility criteria, outcome definitions, or
     575  primary analytical strategy.                                              
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     576  First, to model statistical dependence among effect sizes, we coded the comparison
                                                                                    
     577  structure of each effect size as either independent or dependent (within-subject/repeated-
     578  measures). This expansion of our preregistered metadata structure allowed for accurate
                                                                                    
     579  sampling-variance specification when a single experimental cohort yielded multiple effect
                                                                                    
     580  sizes.                                                                    
                                                                                    
     581  Second, to harmonize heterogeneous reporting, we classified exposure durations (acute,
                                                                                    
     582  short-term, medium-term, or long-term) and grouped music stimuli into broad meta-
     583  genres based on historical context and dominant musical features (see Table 1 for
                                                                                    
     584  definitions). We derived these classifications from the reported text to facilitate
                                                                                    
     585  interpretable moderator analyses.                                         
                                                                                    
     586  Third, we recorded whether studies explicitly reported key stimulus parameters, such as
                                                                                    
     587  loudness, frequency, and light–dark phases. Because loudness and frequency were
                                                                                    
     588  frequently under-reported, we explicitly coded instances of non-reporting instead of
     589  treating them as missing data, allowing us to quantify reporting gaps.    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     590  Fourth, we introduced a post hoc critical appraisal of the included evidence using the
     591  Critical Appraisal of Methodological (technical) Quality, Quality of Reporting and Risk of
                                                                                    
     592  Bias in Animal Research (CRIME-Q) framework (Andersen et al., 2024). We evaluated each
                                                                                    
     593  eligible study against 20 item-level criteria spanning quality of reporting, methodological
     594  quality, and risk of bias.                                                
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     595  Fifth, following concerns about the level of treatment replication, we conducted a post hoc
     596  targeted review of the original exposure protocols to determine whether music was

     682  5.6  Meta-analytic models                                                 
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     683  We fitted multilevel meta-analytic models separately for lnRR and lnVR using restricted
     684  maximum likelihood (REML) estimation in metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010). Because individual
                                                                                    
     685  studies often contributed multiple, non-independent effect sizes (e.g., multiple outcomes,
                                                                                    
     686  assays, or contrasts from the same experimental cohort), we explicitly modeled
     687  dependence among effect sizes in two ways. First, sampling covariances were
                                                                                    
     688  incorporated using a variance–covariance matrix (𝑽) constructed at the cohort level, such
                                                                                    
     689  that effect sizes derived from the same cohort were assumed to have correlated sampling
                                                                                    
     690  errors (with 𝜌 = 0.5 when within-cohort correlations were not reported), yielding a block-
     691  diagonal-structured 𝑽 matrix (Noble et al., 2017; Nakagawa et al., 2022). Second, we

     693  effect size ID to accommodate residual within-study variation among effect sizes beyond
                                                                                    
     694  sampling error, and for strain to control for systematic differences among rodent strains.
                                                                                    
     695  Moderator analyses were conducted as separate uni-moderator meta-regressions using

     756  After the primary analyses, we conducted a post hoc critical appraisal using an adapted
                                                                                    
     757  version of the Critical Appraisal of Methodological (technical) Quality, Quality of Reporting
     758  and Risk of Bias in Animal Research (CRIME-Q) framework (Andersen et al., 2024). We
                                                                                    
     759  assessed 20 reports at the study level using 20 items across three domains: quality of
                                                                                    
     760  reporting (QoR), methodological quality (MQ), and risk of bias (RoB). We omitted CRIME-Q
     761  bench-top or laboratory-procedure items that were not applicable to these whole-animal
                                                                                    
     762  music-exposure experiments.                                               
                                                                                    
     763  QoR items evaluated whether reports provided sufficient detail for replication and
                                                                                    
     764  appraisal, including peer-review status, animal characteristics, music-exposure
                                                                                    
     765  procedures, animal-welfare compliance, blinding, methods–results congruency, study
     766  limitations, and conflicts of interest. MQ items evaluated whether the animals and music-
                                                                                    
     767  exposure design were appropriate for the stated behavioral question, including group
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
          33                                                                        
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
### PAGE 34
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     768  comparability, sample-size justification, acoustic delivery, exposure fidelity, control
     769  conditions, and potential acoustic cross-contamination. RoB items examined baseline
                                                                                    
     770  balance (whether relevant pre-intervention animal or group characteristics were
                                                                                    
     771  comparable between music and control groups), allocation sequence generation, housing
                                                                                    
     772  or cage-position effects, outcome-assessment selection rules, experimenter or handler
     773  blinding, outcome-assessor blinding, attrition, selective outcome reporting, and potential
                                                                                    
     774  funder or commercial influence.                                           
                                                                                    
     775  Each item was scored using its prespecified CRIME-Q response options. Reporting and
                                                                                    
     776  methodological-quality items were generally scored as Yes, Partly, or No; risk-of-bias
                                                                                    
     777  items were generally scored as Yes, No, or Unclear, with Not applicable available only for
     778  items where the codebook allowed it. We retained ratings as item-level categorical data
                                                                                    
     779  and did not combine them into an overall quality score. S.O. performed the initial appraisal
                                                                                    
     780  using the original reports and a study-specific CRIME-Q coding manual. NotebookLM (3.5
     781  Flash) was then used as a source-grounded extraction aid to cross-check each item
                                                                                    
     782  against the primary studies, the adapted CRIME-Q codebook, and the coding rules. The AI
                                                                                    
     783  output was treated as provisional: all discrepancies and uncertain ratings were resolved by
     784  rechecking the original sources. Finally, A.L. independently cross-checked a random
                                                                                    
     785  sample of two studies (10% of reports). The adapted CRIME-Q codebook, including the
                                                                                    
     786  item definitions, response options, and operational decision rules used in the appraisal, is
                                                                                    
     787  provided in Supplementary Material. The NotebookLM prompts and instructions,
     788  supporting workflow files, and finalized study-level assessment matrix are available in the
                                                                                    
     789  project repository at https://github.com/Santiago-                        
                                                                                    
     790  0rtega/Music_on_anxiety_depression_meta_analysis. The CRIME-Q workflow files are also
     791  included in the Borealis archive. The codebook defines the appraisal items and scoring
                                                                                    
     792  rules; the calibration rules guide uncertain ratings; the general instructions describe
                                                                                    
     793  source-grounded checks; and the wide-sheet template defines the assessment columns.
     794  The files can be accessed directly at https://github.com/Santiago-

     797  5.10 Statistical computing environment and software                       
                                                                                    
                                                                                    
     798  All analyzes and visualizations were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2025) (version 4.5.2)
     799  using RStudio (Posit team 2026) (version 2026.01.0+392). We fitted the meta-analytic
                                                                                    
     800  models using metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010)(version 4.8-0). Orchard plots were generated
                                                                                    
     801  using orchaRd (Nakagawa, Lagisz, et al. 2023) (version 2.1.3), figures were constructed
     802  with ggplot2 (Wickham, 2011) (version 4.0.1) and combined using patchwork (Pendersen,
                                                                                    
     803  2025) (version 1.3.2). Data preprocessing used tidyverse (Wickham et al., 2019)
                                                                                    
     804  (version 2.0.0). Post hoc comparisons among moderator levels were performed using
                                                                                    
     805  multcomp (Bretz et al., 2010) (version 1.4-29). All data processing, effect size calculations
     806  (ln𝑅𝑅 and ln𝑉𝑅), model specifications, diagnostic checks, bias assessments, and full
                                                                                    
     807  moderator outputs are provided in a fully reproducible online supplementary document
                                                                                    
     808  available at: https://santiago-                                           
     809  0rtega.github.io/Music_on_anxiety_depression_meta_analysis/. The website includes
                                                                                    
     810  annotated R code, complete model summaries, and figures.
