Lemercier (2000) concluded that environmen- tal enrichment, such as by short-duration music, decreases the rat’s reactivity in housing spaces but only has a lit- tle influence when rats are taken out to be handled and has no influence on the results obtained during an anxi- ety test (Elevated Plus-Maze). However, Rauscher (2006) show a neurophysiological basis for a Mozart effect in rats to complete a T-maze more quickly if they had been reared listening to a Mozart piano sonata, a conclusion that is con- tested by Steele (2006), who argued that the rats were deaf to most of the notes of the sonata. However, expo- sure to Mozart’s music early in life can increase BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) concentration in the hippocampus in rats (Marzban et al., 2011). Chikahisa et al. (2007) hypothesized that exposure to Mozart’s music will reduce anxiety levels in mice and that gender differences might be observed related to ovarian steroid hormones. The ovarian steroid hormones, estrogen and progesterone, have been well documented as having an association with anxiety levels (Joffe and Cohen, 1998; Schmidt et al., 2000; Koss et al., 2004; Saavedra et al., 2006). The results of Chikahisa et al. (2007) suggest that exposure to music reduces anxiety levels in mice, and ovar- ian steroids, mainly progesterone, may be involved in this effect. The anxiolytic-like action of estradiol and progesterone administration in ovariectomized (OVX) female rats has been demonstrated in an elevated plus-maze test (Bitran et al., 1993; Koss et al., 2004), but not in a light/dark transition test. To be specific, the psychotropic effects observed after progesterone administration are due to the bioconversion of progesterone to allopregnanolone, which subsequently augments GABA(A) receptor-mediated func- tion (Bitran et al., 1993), via a nongenomic mechanism (Bitran et al., 1995). This bioconversion is mediated by 5- (cid:3)-reductase enzyme, which modulates the estrous cycle variations, producing a lower anxiety in proestrous for the elevated plus maze test than in diestrous, when the enzyme dose is lower (Koonce et al., 2012). The possible anxiolytic action of direct allopregnalonone administration in OVX rats has been assayed in the burying behaviour test (Fernandez-Guasti and Picazo, 1995), but not in the elevated plus maze and light-dark box transition tests. We therefore hypothesized in rats that: (i) the Sonata for 2 pianos K. 448 by W.A. Mozart could reduce anxiety levels in two behavioural tests (elevated plus maze and light–dark box transition tests), in opposition to white noise and silence. Also, we hypothesized (ii) a role in anxiety reduc- tion of the estrogens, progesterone and allopregnalonone administration in OVX rats in the elevated plus maze and light–dark box transition tests. Finally, we assayed as anxi- olytics (iii) the role of ovarian steroid hormones with two inhibitors, one of 5-(cid:3)-reductase and another of estrogen receptors, in rats with intact ovaries. 2. Material and methods 2.1. Animals and treatments Female Wistar rats (200–250 g) three months old and with regular ovarian cycles were used in this The study. rats, housed in groups of three animals in a plas- tic cage (524 × 274 × 150 mm), were fed ad lib (Purina®, Barcelona) and maintained in a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 08:00 h) at a controlled temperature (21–23 ◦C). All behavioural testing was completed between 13:00 and 17:00 h, and rats were used only once for each type of behavioural test. The experiments were approved by the Bioethics Committee of Córdoba University and carried out according to the guidelines of the Directive of 24 November 1986 (86/609/ECC) approved by the European Communi- ties Council. The experiments carried out were: Experiment 1: In this study the role of Mozart’s music, noise and silence, in anxiety was investigated. Control rats were randomly assigned to one of three groups: (i) silence group, (ii) white noise group and (iii) Mozart music group, with n = 6 rats per group. In the music or white-noise group, rats were exposed to music or white noise from 30 to 45 min before the behavioural tests and also during the test (Chikahisa et al., 2007). Music (Mozart’s 2 piano sonata, K. 448) was played repeatedly on a CD player, and white noise (sound that has all frequencies) was reproduced by a stereo radio, which was not tuned to any particular station (frequency of 300–10,000 Hz) both apparatus situated at 70–100 cm from the rats (Lin et al., 2013). The silence con- dition was maintained in the same room as the music and white noise groups, where there was no sound except ambient noise. The sound level of the silence group was 55 dB, and 65 dB for music and white noise in the home cages and in the behavioural apparatus with the sound level measured by a decibelmeter (PCE-222®, PCE Ibérica S.L., Spain). Experiment 2: This was designed to verify the role of ovarian steroid hormones in the anxiogenic effects of white noise and the anxiolytic ones of music detected in exper- iment 1. Rats were ovariectomized (OVX, n = 18) or sham operated (SO, n = 18). After a 14 day recovery period, the rats were randomly assigned to one of six groups (n = 6 per group): (i) SO-silence; (ii) SO-white noise; (iii) SO- music (Mozart’s 2 piano sonata, K. 448); (iv) OVX-silence; (v) OVX-white noise; (vi) OVX-music. The three stimuli (silence, white noise and music) were applied with the same protocol of experiment 1. Experiment 3: To establish which ovarian steroid hor- mones were involved in anxiety levels, in experiment 3 in OVX rats, they were injected with 17-(cid:2)-estradiol (17(cid:2)e), progesterone (P) and allopregnanolone (allo-p). 0.50 mg/kg weight/day (w.d) for 17(cid:2)e, 0.50 mg/kg w.d for P and 250 (cid:4)g/kg w.d for allo-p (metabolite of the pro- gesterone) were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) in OVX rats during the three days before the behavioural test- ing. Dimethylsulphoxide (DMSO) was administered s.c. to control animals. Behavioural testing was performed 1 h after last administration s.c. to animals. 12 groups were established (n = 6 per group): (i) OVX + DMSO-silence; (ii) OVX + DMSO-white noise; (iii) OVX + DMSO-music; (iv) OVX + 17(cid:2)E-silence; (v) OVX + 17(cid:2)E-white noise; (vi) OVX + 17(cid:2)E-music (Mozart’s 2 piano sonata, K. 448); (vii) OVX + P-silence; (viii)) OVX + P-white noise; (ix) OVX + P- music; (x) OVX + allo-p-silence; (xi) OVX + allo-p-white noise; (xii) OVX + allo-p-music.

Behavioural testing Two behavioural tests were made: Elevated plus maze and Light–dark box transition: Elevated plus maze: Rats were placed in the centre of an apparatus consisting of two opposing runways (+form). One of the runways contained the closed arms (10 × 50 cm) of the maze, which had walls 30 cm high. The other run- way consisted of the open arms (10 × 30 cm) of the maze, which had no walls. The maze was raised to 50 cm above the floor. Behaviour of the rats in the maze was monitored for 5 min. The number of entries and time spent in the open arms were recorded automatically. Increased time spent in the open arms and a major number of entries have shown themselves to be an index of lowered anxiety behaviour (Lister, 1987; Chikahisa et al., 2007). Light–dark box transition D: A chamber divided equally into two compartments (44 × 48 × 50 cm) was used for the light–dark transition test. The light compartment consisted of a white floor and transparent walls and a lid. The dark compartment consisted of a black floor, walls, and a lid and was completely enclosed except for a small (15 × 10 cm) opening to allow movement from the dark compartment to the light one. Rats were placed in the dark compartment and latency in going into the light side for the first time was recorded. The amount of time spent in each compartment was also recorded for 5 min. A greater amount of time spent in the light side of the chamber and a shorter latency have been shown to be an index of lowered anxiety behaviour (Bourin and Hascoet, 2003; Chikahisa et al., 2007; Kushnir et al., 2012). These anxiety tests were recorded digitally in a Sony Handycam® DCR-DVD 105 videocamera, located in the chamber in which the two behaviour tests were performed. 2.3. Measurement of plasma levels of 17 ˇ-estradiol and progesterone The animals were sacrificed after the behavioural tests, and trunk blood was collected for measurement of plasma levels of 17 (cid:2)-estradiol and progesterone. concentrations Plasma of 17 (cid:2)-estradiol and progesterone were determined by ELISA. 2.4. Statistical analyses All values were expressed as mean ± S.E. The normality of the variables was evaluated with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Behavioural tests were ana- lyzed by a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and corrected with Bonferroni-test with a SPSSwin15 (IBM® SPSS® software) statistic programme. Discussion Routine procedures in laboratory animal facilities include moving animals from a dirty to a new clean cage–cage cleaning – and transporting cages with animals inside a room or between rooms – in-house transport. Even being usually simple and relatively quick proce- dures, several authors have shown that they induce acute stress responses in laboratory animals (Castelhano-Carlos and Baumans, 2009). Environmental enrichment has been shown to reduce both anxiety and stress-related behaviour (Ravenelle et al., 2013). From the perspective of the lab animal facility, enrichment is vital to improving animal welfare, but facility staff should be aware of its impact research on and ensure a consistent and continual supply to manage that impact (Wersinger and Martin, 2009). Our findings demonstrate the anxiogenic effect of noise compared to the anxiolytic effect of a Mozart piano sonata on rats submitted to two behaviour tests (elevated plus maze and light–dark box transition) and the implication of ovarian hormones in anxiety reinforcing the anxiolytic role of the Mozart music and of silence versus noise in ovariectomized rats. The results obtained in experiment 1 confirmed the anx- iolytic effect of the piece of music selected with respect to silence, in agreement with the data observed by Chikahisa et al. (2007) for the elevated plus maze test. This anxiolytic effect of the 2 piano sonata K 448 by Mozart is contrary to the anxiogenic effect generated by white noise and dif- fers from that contrasted by Chikahisa et al. (2007) in their experiment with mice, or what was reported by Rex et al. (2005), in whose experiment mild stress measured by sero- tonin release in ventral hippocampus was produced. The “Mozart effect”, was initially described by Rauscher et al. (1993), when compared with the same period of silence or relaxation instruction. Reports of the Stanford—Binet Intelligence Scale show that students scored nine points higher on spatial tasks after listening to Mozart’s Sonata for Two Pianos in D Major, K.448 (Mozart K. 448). Laster, Lin et al. (2013) suggested that Mozart’s sonata had a positive short-term effect in attenuating the spon- taneous high-voltage rhythmic spike discharges in Long Evans rats. Akiyama and Sutoo (2011) demonstrated that the blood pressure-reducing response was dependent on the frequency of the sonata K 448 by Mozart, and was markedly greater at 4–16 kHz compared with lower fre- quencies (average sound level was maintained at 65 dB in each frequency range). In another experiment realized by Nún˜ez et al. (2002), music treatment, produced by playing the Herbert Von Karajan Adagio compact disk through loudspeakers (at less than 40 dB) from 09:00 h until 14:00 h daily during eight days, has been seen to decrease the metastaticity of WRC 256 cells in Sprague-Dawley male rats of both groups: submitted and non-submitted to auditory stress (exposing animals to the fire alarm bell (100 dB)). To establish which ovarian steroid hormones are involved in anxiety levels, experiment 3 in OVX rats inject- ing them with 17(cid:2)e, P and allo-p was carried out. Diverse experiments have already demonstrated the anxiolytic role of these hormones (Fernandez-Guasti and Picazo, 1995; Palanza et al., 2001; Frye and Walf, 2002; Koss et al., 2004). Chikahisa et al. (2007) in addition, suggest that P may be involved in the anxiolytic effect of music in female mice, while the implantation of a 17(cid:2)e pellet had no effect. In our study, all the steroid ovarian hormones were injected during three days, unlike what happened in the study of Chikahisa et al. (2007), who employed pellets from which the substance was released during 21 days, which probably explains the differences obtained in our study. In the lat- ter, all the ovarian steroid hormones played an anxiolytic role with respect to the OVX rats, both in the silence and the music phases, and only in the number of entries in the open arms of the elevated plus maze test was the effect of 17(cid:2)e not significant for either of the two phases. This anx- iolytic effect of the ovarian steroid hormones in the case of music did not differ from that of silence and did not reach the heights detected in control rats. Finally, in intact rats, it was attempted to establish the connection between ovarian steroid hormones and their psychotropic effects on anxiety, counting for this on two inhibitors, one of P activity (finasteride) and another of 17(cid:2)e (faslodex) and how they influenced the response to the different stimuli programmed. Thus, finasteride was used as an inhibitor of the enzyme 5-(cid:3)-reductase, a converter of P into allop. This medicament caused anxiogenic effects in the silence and music groups with respect to the treated control group, except for the number of entries in the open arms in the first case. Also in the case of the elevated plus maze test for the time spent in the open arms, finasteride triggered an increase in anxiety in the rats submitted to white noise. The role of finasteride as an inhibitor of the anxiolytic effect produced by allo-p has been contrasted in diverse studies (Brito Faturi et al., 2006). These results, therefore, abounded in the relation of P and allo-p to the diminution of anxiety in rats submitted to silence and music, in agreement with what was obtained by Chikahisa et al. (2007) in mice for the latter stimulus, and the opposite to that postulated by these authors for the former one. Finasteride also seems to have an anxio- genic effect on rats submitted to white noise for the time spent in the open arms test in the elevated plus maze test. However, this effect was not strengthened or eliminated in ovariectomized rats, or when the latter were submitted to treatments with P or allo-p. This fact seems to confirm the anxiogenic effect of noise due to different mechanisms of influence from those of the ovarian steroid hormones. This may be attributed to increased brain serotonin lev- els and decreased dopamine levels following sub-chronic noise exposure as suggested by Naqvi et al. (2012) for male rats. With regard to the use of faslodex as an estrogenic inhibitor, although the role of 17(cid:2)e as a participant in the anxiolytic response in different elevated plus maze and light–dark box transition tests for the stimuli silence music and has been verified, only in the case of the latter has it been possible to note the effects of an increase in anxiety with respect to control rats. Significant differences in the use of faslodex were even established for the time spent in the light side test with the use of finasteride, which causes a much larger diminution in the effects of music on anxi- ety, resulting in a greater anxiogenic effect. This seems to imply that estrogens, despite being involved in the anxi- ety levels, have a lesser influence than P and allo-p, whose action could mask the inhibition of estrogen receptors. This was contrary to what was reported by Koss et al. (2004), who do not observe any reversal of the anxiogenic effects of estradiol from an acute administration of P in a social interaction test in OVX rats. In conclusion, this study has confirmed the anxiogenic effect of noise and the anxiolytic one of music, along with the ability of the rats to distinguish both stimuli despite both being given at dB (65 dB). However, although ovariec- tomy did not influence the capacity of noise for triggering anxiety, it did so in the silence and music effects, although the latter went on exerting its anxiolytic action (albeit reduced) separately from the influence of ovarian steroid hormones. The administration of these hormones reduced anxiety in OVX rats only in the case of silence and music, so that noise must apply its anxiogenic effect by other differ- ent mechanisms from those of ovarian steroid hormones. Also, among the latter, P and allop are the most influential ones in reducing anxiety levels. The noise level in animal houses and laboratories can be sufficient enough to act as a stressor (Castelhano-Carlos and Baumans, 2009). From this point of view, the find- ings in this paper show that exposure to music (Mozart’s 2 piano sonata K 448) of only 65 dB exerts its anxiolytic power compared to noises of the same intensity and that it is recognizable by female laboratory rats. Furthermore, the power of Mozart’s music, although dependent on the ovarian hormones, continues to exercise its function in ovariectomized females, so that the environmental enrich- ment bestowed by music is also valid in these cases. However, the effect of background noise, such as the radio and different types of music, in masking the effects of ultra- sound and loud noises needs further investigation.
