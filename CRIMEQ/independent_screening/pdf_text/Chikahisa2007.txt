Abstract
Music is known to be able to elicit emotional changes, including anxiolytic effects. The gonadal steroid hormones estradiol and progesterone have
also been reported to play important roles in the modulation of anxiety. In the present study, we examined whether the effect of music on anxiety
is related to ovarian steroid in female mice. Behavioral paradigms measuring anxiety were tested in gonadally intact (SHAM) and ovariectomized
(OVX) female mice chronically treated with either placebo (OVX/Placebo), 17/H9252-estradiol (OVX/E), or progesterone (OVX/P). In the elevated plus
maze, light–dark transition, and marble burying tests, SHAM and OVX/P mice exposed to music showed less anxiety than those exposed to white
noise or silence while OVX/placebo mice did not show these effects at all. OVX/E mice showed the anxiolytic effect of music only in the marble
burying test. Furthermore, pretreatment with progesterone’s metabolite inhibitor completely prevented the anxiolytic effect of music in behavioral
tests, while pretreatment with a progesterone receptor blocker did not prevent the anxiolytic effect of music. These results suggest that exposure
to music reduces anxiety levels, and ovarian steroids, mainly progesterone, may be involved in the anxiolytic effect of music observed in female
mice.

1. Introduction
Music has been well known to elicit emotional changes,
including anxiolytic effects in humans. In addition to psycholog-
ical mood changes, music has been shown to elicit physiological
changes in heart rate, blood pressure, and so forth [44]. There
have been some clinical ﬁndings indicating that listening to
music is useful in reducing anxiety [8] and in diminishing
stress-induced increases in blood pressure related to surgery
[1]. Several studies have demonstrated gender differences in
response to musical stimuli in humans. In females, but not males,
pain intensity and unpleasantness ratings were affected by the
tempo of the music, accompanied by arousal changes[20]. Fur-
thermore, it has been reported that women display heightened
responses to an arousing and unpleasant stimulus while men did

perception remain to be elucidated.
Studies in rodents have also indicated that there are gen-
der differences in emotional behavior caused by environmental
conditions. Different social environments have been shown to
exhibit different inﬂuences on male and female mice [30].I n
particular, individual housing reduced reactivity and exploration
and increased anxiety levels only in female mice [31]. Females
have also been shown to exhibit higher plasma concentrations of
ACTH and corticosterone and a greater magnitude and duration
of hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) response to different
stressors [35]. In rats, females have been shown to exhibit less
anxiety-like behavior than males [19]. However, closer studies
of these behaviors during the estrous cycle have shown that this
reduced anxiety-like behavior generally occurs during the stage
of proestrous. Proestrous rats entered more central squares of the
open ﬁeld than diestrous female or male rats[13] and spent more

The emotional changes in female rodents are considered to
be associated with ovarian steroid hormones [30]. The ovar-
ian steroid hormones estrogen and progesterone have been well
documented to have an association with anxiety levels. Mood
ﬂuctuations, depression, and anxiety have often been related to
low levels of estradiol in peri- and post-menopausal women,
whereas estrogen replacement therapy ameliorated these psy-
chological conditions [18,39]. In ovariectomized female rats,
subchronic estrogen treatment produced an anxiolytic-like effect
in the elevated plus maze, while it produced anxiogenic-like
effect in the social interaction test [21]. The effects of estrogen
are mediated by its two known speciﬁc receptors, estrogen recep-
tor /H9251(ER/H9251) and /H9252(ER/H9252) [3]. It has been reported that random
cycling female ER /H9252-deﬁcient mice exhibited increased anxi-
ety compared to their wild-type littermates and ER /H9251-deﬁcient
mice, and that ER/H9252-deﬁcient mice also had disrupted GABAer-
gic function in the medial amygdala [22]. Thus, estradiol has
important roles in the modulation of anxiety level.
Another ovarian hormone, progesterone, has also been impli-
cated in anxiety. Withdrawal of chronic progesterone exposure,
which is thought to be related to premenstrual anxiety in some
women, has been reported to elicit an anxiogenic effect in
a defensive burying test [15,37] and in the acoustic startle
response [17] in female rats. These responses were accompa-
nied by up-regulation of the GABA
A receptor /H92514 subunit in the
amygdala [17]. Furthermore, many studies using various animal
paradigms have indicated that treatment with progesterone or its
metabolites produced anxiolytic-like behavior [4,5,12–14,33].
Progesterone has a genomic mechanism of action via proges-
terone receptors (PR), PR-A and PR-B, and a non-genomic
mechanism of action through the activation of the GABA
A-
receptor via progesterone metabolites[3]. Through the genomic
mechanism, the PR has been reported to play a critical role for
the expression of sexual behavior in female mice [26]. Most
researchers have linked the anxiolytic effect of progesterone to
the nongenomic mechanism[5,12–14,33]. Progesterone is read-
ily metabolized by 5/H9251-reductase to dihydroprogesterone (DHP),
which is then metabolized by 3/H9251-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase
to 3 /H9251-hydroxy-5/H9251-pregnan-20-one (allopregnanolone), a very
effective modulator of GABA
A receptor complex [3]. Proges-
terone enhanced anxiolytic activity in wild-type mice but not in
5/H9251-reductase-deﬁcient mice in the open ﬁeld [14] although the
anxiolytic action of progesterone was observed in both isoforms
of PR knockout mice in the elevated plus maze [33].
Thus, the ovarian steroid hormones estrogen and proges-
terone have been shown to be closely involved in anxiety levels
in both human and rodents. We, therefore, hypothesized that
if exposure to music reduces anxiety levels in mice as well as
humans, gender differences might be observed, and these gender
differences would be related to ovarian steroid hormones. In the
present study, we examined whether exposure to music reduces
anxiety levels in mice and investigated the possible contribu-
tion of ovarian steroid hormones to the anxiolytic mechanism of
exposure to music in mice.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Animals and treatments
Mice of the Slc:ddY strain (Slc Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) were used in this
study. The mice were fed ad lib and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle
(lights on at 0800 h) at a controlled ambient temperature (24 ± 1
◦C). All sur-
gical manipulations were conducted under appropriate anesthesia (Ketamine
100 mg/kg + xylazine 25 mg/kg i.p.). All behavioral testing was completed
between 1300 and 1700 h, and mice were used only once for each type of behav-
ioral test. The experiments were approved by the Animal Study Committee of
Tokushima University (No. 05047) and carried out according to the guidelines
for the Care and Use of Animals approved by the Council of the Physiological
Society of Japan.
2.2. Experiment 1: ovariectomy and pellet implantation
Female mice (25–32 g) were ovariectomized (OVX, n = 116) or sham-
operated (SHAM, n = 48) at the age of 8 weeks. The female mice were group
housed, but only two mice were placed in each cage (22 cm× 16 cm× 12 cm) in
order to produce a varied estrus cycle per cage. After a 2-week recovery period,
21-day-release pellets (IRA, USA) that delivered approximately 2.4/H9262g/day for
17/H9252-estradiol (OVX/E,n = 38), 240/H9262g/day for progesterone (OVX/P,n = 40), or
placebo pellets (OVX/Placebo,n = 38) were implanted into OVX-animals subcu-
taneously (s.c.). During the following 7 days, mice were handled for 5 min once
a day between 1300 and 1700 h. Behavioral testing started 8 days and ﬁnished 15
days after implantation. One type of behavioral test was completed every other
day. In the behavioral tests, surgically treated mice were randomly assigned to
one of three groups: silence (silence group), white noise (white-noise group), and
music (music group) with approximatelyn = 13 per group for each hormonal con-
dition. In the music or white-noise group, mice were exposed to music or white
noise from 30 to 45 min before behavioral tests, and also during the tests. Music
(Mozart’s piano sonata, K. 448) was played repeatedly on a CD player, and white
noise was produced by radio which was not tuned to a particular station. The
silence condition was performed in the same room as the music and white-noise
groups where there was no sound except ambient noise, such as that produced
by the air conditioner. The sound level of the silence group was 55 dB, music
65–75 dB, and the white noise 70 dB in the home cages and in the behavioral
apparatus.
2.3. Experiment 2: drug treatment
Finasteride (LKT Laboratories, USA), a 5 /H9251-reductase inhibitor that blocks
the conversion of progesterone to allopregnanolone, or mifepristone (RU38486)
(Sigma, USA), a PR antagonist, was administered to 9-week-old female mice
(26–34 g) with intact ovaries. Finasteride and mifepristone were dissolved in
sesame oil and administered s.c. at a dose of 50 mg/kg (ﬁnasteride) or 20 mg/kg
(mifepristone), respectively. The doses of these drugs were chosen to match
those effectively used in previous studies examining progesterone and anxiety
in rodents [5,13,33]. A sesame oil vehicle was administered s.c. to control ani-
mals. Behavioral testing was performed 2 h after administration of each drug
using the same procedures as the hormonal experiments mentioned above. The
mice treated with each drug were randomly assigned to one of three groups:
silence group, white-noise group and music group, approximately n = 14 per
group.
2.4. Behavioral testing
2.4.1. Open ﬁeld
Locomotor behavior of mice was videotaped in an open ﬁeld test chamber.
The chamber was an open top box (45 cm × 45 cm× 30 cm) made of acrylic.
Behavior of the mice in the chamber was monitored for 10 min. The total dis-
tance and time spent in the central and peripheral area were recorded using an
automated image analysis system (Smart System, Pan Lab s.l., Spain). Increased
time spent in the central area has been shown to be an index of lowered anxiety
behavior [32].\n[p.3] 52 S. Chikahisa et al. / Behavioural Brain Research 179 (2007) 50–59
2.4.2. Elevated plus maze
Mice were placed in the center of an apparatus consisting of two opposing
runways. One of the runways contained the closed arms (6 cm × 30 cm) of the
maze, which had walls 15 cm high. The other runway consisted of the open
arms (7 cm× 30 cm) of the maze, which had no walls. The maze was elevated
to 70 cm above the ﬂoor. Behavior of the mice in the maze was monitored for
5 min. The number of entries and time spent in the open arms were recorded
automatically. Increased time spent in the open arms has been shown to be an
index of lowered anxiety behavior [25].
2.4.3. Light–dark transition
A chamber divided equally into two compartments (22 cm× 24 cm× 30 cm)
was used for the light–dark transition test. The light compartment consisted
of white ﬂoor and transparent walls and lid. The dark compartment consisted
of black ﬂoor, walls, and lid and was completely enclosed except for a small
(15 cm× 5 cm) opening to allow movement from the dark compartment to the
light compartment. Mice were placed in the dark compartment, and the latency
to go out into the light side for the ﬁrst time was recorded. The amount of time
spent in each compartment was also recorded for 5 min. A greater amount of
time spent in the light side of the chamber has been shown to be an index of
lowered anxiety behavior [6].
2.4.4. Marble burying test
Marble burying was measured by placing a single mouse in a polypropylene
cage (22 cm × 16 cm× 12 cm) containing 15 glass marbles (1.5 cm diameter)
evenly spaced on 5 cm deep sawdust. No food or water was present during the
observation period. Mice have a tendency to bury objects, such as glass marbles,
present in their environment. It has been suggested that inhibition of marble
burying behavior is correlated with anxiolytic-like activity [29]. Cages were
covered with a metal grid, and the number of marbles at least two-thirds covered
was counted after 30 min.
2.5. Measurement of plasma levels of 17 β-estradiol and
progesterone
The animals were sacriﬁced 2 days after the behavioral tests (17 days after
pellet implantation), and trunk blood was collected for measurement of plasma
levels of 17/H9252-estradiol and progesterone. Plasma concentrations of 17/H9252-estradiol
and progesterone were determined by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay
(ECLIA).
2.6. Statistical analyses
All values are expressed as mean ± S.E.M. Behavioral tests were
analyzed by Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by the post hoc multiple
Mann–Whitney U-test. Plasma concentration of 17 /H9252-estradiol and proges-
terone was analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOV A) followed by Scheffe’s
post hoc test. For all comparisons, the criterion for signiﬁcance was
p < 0.05.
3. Results
3.1. Difference in the effect of music on anxiety levels
between male and female mice in the elevated plus maze
First, the effect of music on anxiety levels was compared
between normal male and female mice using the elevated plus
maze. In the elevated plus maze, total distance in the closed
and open arms did not differ among the three acoustic groups
in both male and female mice ( Fig. 1A). In the parameters of
anxiety, female mice showed signiﬁcant differences among the
three groups in running distance in the open arms ( H = 10.173,
p < 0.01), time spent in the open arms (H = 11.919,p < 0.01), and
the number of entries into the open arms ( H = 9.899, p < 0.01)
while male mice showed no differences among the three acous-
tic groups for any parameter. In female mice, the music group
exhibited greater running distance in the open arms, spent more
time in the open arms, and had a greater number of entries into
the open arms compared to the silence and white-noise groups
(p < 0.01, Fig. 1B–D).

4. Discussion
This study investigated whether exposure to music alters anx-
iety levels in mice and attempted to clarify its mechanism. An
anxiolytic effect of music in the elevated plus maze was shown in
female but not male mice. We, therefore, investigated the possi-
bility that music and ovarian steroid hormones interact to reduce
anxiety. Exposure to music reduced anxiety levels in mice with
intact ovaries. SHAM mice exposed to music increased time
spent in the open arms of the elevated plus maze, time in the light\n[p.8] S. Chikahisa et al. / Behavioural Brain Research 179 (2007) 50–59 57
compartment of the light–dark transition, and buried fewer mar-
bles, although total activity in both the open ﬁeld and the elevated
plus maze was not altered. These effects of music were abolished
by ovariectomy. However, when a pellet of progesterone was
implanted, the anxiolytic effect of music was recovered, while
implantation of a 17/H9252-estradiol pellet was not able to compen-
sate. These data suggest that progesterone may be involved in
the anxiolytic effect of music in female mice.
Progesterone affects the function of the central nervous sys-
tem via two fundamentally different ways; one is mediated
through its metabolites, the other through the PR [3]. In this
study, mice treated with mifepristone, a PR antagonist, contin-
ued to show the anxiolytic effect of music in the behavioral
tests. In contrast, ﬁnasteride, a 5/H9251-reductase inhibitor that blocks
the conversion of progesterone to allopregnanolone, completely
prevented the progesterone-mediated anxiolytic effect of music.
Allopregnanolone, which is a neurosteroid and a metabolite of
progesterone, does not interact with PRs and can rapidly inﬂu-
ence brain excitability as a result of its activity as a potent
positive allosteric modulator of the GABA
A receptor [23,36].
Studies in various animal models of anxiety have demon-
strated that allopregnanolone has anxiolytic activity. Proestrus
rats, which have high progesterone levels, that were adminis-
tered ﬁnasteride showed signiﬁcantly increased anxiety levels
compared to rats administered vehicle [13]. In ovariectomized
rats, 5 /H9251-reductase inhibitors, such as ﬁnasteride, were potent
in preventing the anxiolytic effect observed after progesterone
administration, which was accompanied by increased GABA-
stimulated Cl
− uptake [5]. Furthermore, allopregnanolone levels
change with behavioral and/or environmental stimuli, and the
sensitivity of the biosynthesis of allopregnanolone in response
to these stimuli is greater in females than males [12].I n
studies of environmental stimuli and anxiety, an enriched envi-
ronment decreases anxiety and attenuates stress responses in
mice [2]. An enriched environment is deﬁned as the combi-
nation of complex inanimate and social stimulation and the
interaction of multiple environmental factors, rather than any
single element, that contributes to various brain function in
rodents [42]. Sound is thought to be one of the environmen-
tal factors, and music can also be a signiﬁcant element for
an “enriched” environment in comparison to silence and white
noise. It is considered that complex sound, such as music, alters
the metabolism of progesterone in the brain, which may work
as an anxiolytic factor. In other words, it is possible that the
exposure to music may stimulate the metabolic pathway of
progesterone, which is inhibited by ﬁnasteride, and, thus, acti-
vate the GABA
A receptor in mice with adequate progesterone
levels.
In this study, the stage of the estrus cycle differed in the
SHAM mice tested in experiment 1. Plasma progesterone is
higher during proestrus and from metestrus to diestrus peri-
ods in rodents [7,38,41]. Musical stimulation may, therefore,
affect the SHAM mice mainly during the proestrus, metestrus,
or diestrus periods. All responses caused by exposure to the
three types of acoustic conditions observed in the OVX/P mice
were almost the same as that of the SHAM mice in experiment
1. Therefore, we used intact mice in experiment 2. However,
it also remains to be resolved whether an uncontrolled estrus
cycle in the intact mice may have effects on these results. For
example, vehicle-treated mice unexpectedly did not match the
results in the SHAM mice in the elevated plus maze. In the ele-
vated plus maze, an anxiolytic effect of music was observed
in SHAM mice in experiment 1; however, no effect of music
was observed in vehicle-treated mice as well as ﬁnasteride-
treated mice in experiment 2. This discrepancy may be due to
the mixed estrus cycle in addition to stress induced by injec-
tion in experiment 2. Although we failed to extract an effect of
ﬁnasteride in the elevated plus maze, mifepristone-treated mice
clearly showed an anxiolytic effect of music. It could be, at least
in part, considered that music reduces anxiety level through
a mechanism other than the PR in female mice with intact
ovaries in the elevated plus maze. In a future study, the rela-
tionship between environmental conditions and anxiety levels
in female mice should be investigated under a controlled estrus
cycle.
In the present study, progesterone treatment reduced anxiety
level only in the open ﬁeld test compared with other ovar-
ian treatments in the silence condition. Most previous studies
have demonstrated anxiolytic effects of bolus injection of pro-
gesterone [4,5,14,33]. There have also been a few studies on
the chronic injection of progesterone that showed clear anxi-
olytic effects when compared to vehicle [37]. Different effects
between chronic and acute administration of progesterone on
anxiety levels have been shown[10]. In this study, mice received
chronic administration using pellet implantation in experiment
1 to remove stress caused by multiple injections. It might be
possible that the change in administration conditions from pre-
vious studies may account for the lack of anxiolytic effects of
progesterone in the behavioral tests except for the open ﬁeld
when comparing the silence groups for each surgical and pellet
treatment. However, there were signiﬁcant differences in plasma
levels of progesterone among the four ovarian treatment groups,
which indicates the efﬁcacy of the ovariectomy procedure and
pellet implantation in this study.
In the marble burying test, an anxiolytic effect of music was
observed in OVX/E mice as well as SHAM and OVX/P mice
in experiment 1. Therefore, estradiol may also be involved in
the anxiolytic effect of music to some extent. In experiment 2,
however, this anxiolytic activity caused by music observed in
the marble burying test was completely abolished by ﬁnasteride
in intact mice that were considered to have normal 17/H9252-estradiol
level. In experiment 1, 17 /H9252-estradiol replacement in OVX/E
mice restored 17/H9252-estradiol level to physiologically relevant lev-
els during the proestrus period in female [7,16,38]. In contrast,
the estrus cycle of intact mice used in experiment 2 was mixed as
mentioned above. There is a possibility that a high 17/H9252-estradiol
level is needed as much as the proestrus period to show effects
of music in the marble burying test.
White noise has been reported to be able to evoke a
stress response when it is supplied loudly. Acute exposure
to white noise (20 min of 95 dB) was shown to be a mild
stressor in a behavioral test [34]. Furthermore, plasma corti-
costerone responses to white-noise stimulus (10 min of 114 dB)
in chronically progesterone-treated rats were greater compared\n[p.9] 58 S. Chikahisa et al. / Behavioural Brain Research 179 (2007) 50–59
to progesterone-withdrawn rats [43]. The sound level of white
noise used in the present study was not as loud as these pre-
vious studies but instead supplied at approximately the same
level as that of music. Nevertheless, relative to silence or music,
white noise elicited a signiﬁcant decrease in the percent of time
in the central area of the open ﬁeld in OVX/P (experiment 1).
Similarly, the white-noise group treated with ﬁnasteride spent
less time in the central area of the open ﬁeld compared to
the silence and music groups (experiment 2). Hence, there is
a possibility that quieter white noise could be a stressor hav-
ing no relationship to the metabolism of progesterone in the
open ﬁeld test. In other words, music with ﬂuctuating sound
levels, frequency, and tempo could be less stressful than white
noise.
In the behavioral tests, several factors such as locomotion and
arousal level, may have affected the results. A marked variation
of motor activity and sleep during the course of the estrus cycle
in rodents has been previously observed[40]. Furthermore, sys-
temic administration of progesterone in rats decreased both the
amount of wakefulness and the latency of slow-wave sleep[24].
While it is possible that exposure to music may alter arousal
levels in mice undergoing ovarian hormone treatments in the
present study, OVX/P mice showed no difference among the
three acoustic conditions in the total distance in the central and
peripheral areas in the open ﬁeld and in the total distance in the
open and closed arm in the elevated plus maze; these results sug-
gested that the observed effect of music was not due to changes
in arousal level and basic locomotion.
At the present time, it remains to be clariﬁed whether the
response of mice to music depends on auditory systems or
air oscillations. If the response is mediated by auditory sys-
tems, the sounds from 800 Hz to 20 kHz included in music
are considered to be involved in the anxiolytic effect of music
observed in this study because mice can hear only the portion
of music with frequencies greater than 800 Hz [11]. There is
also a possibility that the effect of music on mice is mediated
not by the auditory system but by air oscillations created by
sound. Environmental stimuli, including somatosensory input,
are reported to be related to anxiety levels in rodents. For exam-
ple, handled animals, especially those that experienced handling
during the postnatal period, were found to be less anxious
than controls in several behavioral tests [9]. Additionally, gen-
der differences in response to these environmental conditions
have been observed. Individually housed females demonstrated
lower exploration and a higher level of anxiety compared to
group-housed females while individually housed males tended
to show the opposite proﬁle [30,31]. In the present study, there-
fore, it is possible that air oscillations produced by sound
may affect anxiety via the somatosensory system in female
mice. We are planning to investigate this problem using deaf
mice.
In summary, exposure to music reduced anxiety levels only in
female mice, which depended on ovarian steroids, mainly pro-
gesterone and its metabolites. This anxiolytic effect of music
mediated by progesterone may also be useful in the treatment
of anxiety during the gestational period or luteal phase, both
of which are times when women have high progesterone lev-
els. Future studies will address the molecular substances that
mediate the anxiolytic effect of music and ovarian steroid.
