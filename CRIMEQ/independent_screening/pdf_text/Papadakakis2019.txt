Music exposure attenuates anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and

ABSTRACT
Epidemiological and clinical studies suggest that early-life stress (ELS) may lead to the development of mental
disorders in adulthood. Maternal separation (MS) is a valid animal model of ELS that produces detrimental
eﬀects on brain and behavior of experimental animals. Positive environmental stimuli have been shown to
counteract the behavioral de ﬁcits of ELS and enhance neuroplasticity. Recent data indicate that music may serve
as a form of environmental enrichment in experimental animals. However, the underlying mechanisms through
which musical enrichment exerts its eﬀ ects are poorly understood. Male Sprague –Dawley rats were submitted to
a 3 h MS protocol during postnatal days (PND) 2 –14, while another group was left undisturbed. Half of the
animals within each group were exposed from PND 21 to PND 76 for 12 h/day to Mozart K. 448. At approxi-
mately three months of age, elevated plus maze procedure, forced swim test and social approach task were
applied to test whether music exposure can mitigate the eﬀ ects of MS stress on animal emotional behaviors.
Moreover, we investigated the eﬀ ects of these treatments on dendritic spine density in the CA1 region of hip-
pocampus. As expected, MS rats showed decreased sociability, increased anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors,
and decreased mature dendritic spines in the CA1 region of hippocampus in adulthood. Musical enrichment
reversed these eﬀ ects. Our results suggest that musical enrichment can reverse the negative eﬀ ects of MS on
anxiety, depression, and sociability in adult rats and modulate neuronal plasticity and provide additional data
for a new therapeutic intervention to rescue emotional symptoms.
1. Introduction
Consistent epidemiological evidence links early adverse experiences
in humans with the development of psychopathology in adulthood
[1,2]. In the same way, exposure to early-life stress in rodents is able to
induce a variety of physiological and behavioral alterations in later life
and has been associated with a range of emotional disorders and im-
paired cognitive performance [ 3,4]. Indeed, the initial period of post-
natal life (for example in rats PND 2 – PND 21) is characterized by great
plasticity and represents a critical development interval where any in-
sult or inappropriate intervention can in ﬂuence both positively and
negatively brain development and behavior [ 5–7].
Maternal separation (MS) is a widely used rodent model of adverse
early-life experience [ 8,9]. Rat pups subjected to repeated separation
from dam develop altered behavioral phenotypes, including anxiety-
and depression-like behavior in adulthood [ 10]. Moreover, MS leads to
increased stress-induced corticosterone levels, blunted
adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) response, reduced hippocampal
glucocorticoid receptors, reduced levels of BDNF in the hippocampus,
loss of dendritic spines and decreased hippocampal neurogenesis
[11,12]. Interestingly, these are long-term changes and persist until
adulthood [ 13].
Environmental enrichment involves the enhancement of an animal ’s
physical or social environment. Examples of environmental enrichment
in rodents include social enrichment with direct or indirect contact with
individuals of the same species or humans, running wheels that en-
courage exercise, physical enrichment with speci ﬁc objects (for ex-
ample nest boxes), sensory enrichment (e.g. auditory stimuli) and nu-
tritional enrichment (e.g. novel food types) [ 14].
Emerging evidence suggests that detrimental e ﬀects of early adverse
experiences, such as MS, could be e ﬀectively reversed by the so-called
"positive environmental stimuli", like environmental enrichment,
sociability and physical exercise [ 15–18]. These stimuli have been
found to reduce aggression, anxiety, fear and stress [ 19], depression-

of the brain alterations induced by MS could be a useful method for the

Musical stimuli may also serve as a form of environmental enrich-
ment in experimental animals. Indeed, music is well known to a ﬀect
human physiology and behavior and regulate mood and emotion and
several studies have examined its clinical application on patients with
anxiety and depression [ 22–24]. Moreover, musical stimuli in humans
activate several brain areas associated with emotional behaviors [ 25],
whereas in experimental animals can induce neuroplastic changes in
several brain regions, such as prefrontal cortex [ 26], amygdala [ 27],
hippocampus [ 28] and hypothalamus [ 29]. Interestingly, music can
ameliorate depression- and anxiety-like behaviors and enhance learning
and memory performance in experimental animals [ 27,30,31].
Although these ﬁndings indicate that music has several positive
behavioral e ﬀects and elicits several neuroplastic changes in the brain,
its potential protective e ﬀect on the behavioral and neural deﬁ cits in-
duced by early life stress has not been studied. Therefore, the present
study utilized the social approach task, the elevated plus maze proce-
dure and the forced swim test to test the hypothesis that exposure to a
patterned sound, such as Mozart ’s sonata K. 448, during early devel-
opment (i.e. from PND 21 to PND 76) is able to mitigate the e ﬀects of
MS stress on animal emotional behaviors. Finally, since the hippo-
campus plays a critical role in emotional processes and shows a con-
siderable amount of plasticity, we investigated the e ﬀects of these
treatments on neuronal spine density in the CA1 region of hippocampus
using the Golgi-Cox staining method.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Animals
Male rat pups were o ﬀspring of experimentally naive
Sprague–Dawley rats from an in-house breeding colony of the
Laboratory of Behavioral Neuroscience, Department of Psychology,
University of Crete. Litters were housed under standard experimental
conditions in a humidity- and temperature-controlled vivarium room on
a 12-h light –12-h dark cycle (lights on from 08:30 to 20:30 h), with
standard rodent chow and water available ad libitum . On postnatal
(PND) day 1, litters were culled to 8– 10 pups (60 –70% male subjects).
On PND 2, rats were randomly divided into two groups: maternal se-
paration group (MS, n = 22) and control group (CG, n = 20). On PND
21, rats were weaned and randomly divided into two groups: maternal
separation group (MS, n = 22) and control group (CG, n = 20). These
two groups of rats were further divided into two sub-groups as follows:
1) MS + Ambient Noise (MSA) (n = 11); 2) MS + Mozart ’s Sonata K.
448 (MSM) (n = 11); 3) Control + Ambient Noise (CGA) (n = 10); 4)
Control + Mozart’s Sonata K. 448 (CGM) (n = 10). Thereafter, animals
were
housed in same-sex and -experimental groups with 2 –3 rats per
cage. Experiments were conducted in accordance with the European
Communities Council Directive (86/609/EEC) and the National
Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.
All e ﬀorts were made to minimize animal su ﬀering and to reduce the
number of animals used.
2.2. Maternal separation (MS)
A MS procedure as previously described by Daniels et al. [ 11], was
implemented. MS was conducted in 5 litters, while 5 other litters re-
mained undisturbed. Thus, rat pups were removed from their dams for
3 h daily (11:00 h – 14:00 h) from PND 2 to PND 14 for half of the litters
(n = 22). During each session of MS, dams were removed from their
home cages and placed in a separate cage ﬁlled with nesting material in
the same room, while pups remained in their home cages. After 3 h,
dams were returned to their home cage and reunited with their litter.
Control dams and litters were left undisturbed during this period, apart
from standard animal facility maintenance cleaning.
2.3. Music exposure
Mozart’s sonata for two pianos in D major, K. 448, which is usually
used in studies on the “Mozart eﬀect” was employed in this study. Two
groups of rats, as previously described, were exposed repeatedly for
12 h per day (20:30 h –08:30 h) to music from PND 21 to PND 76. The
control groups were not exposed to any music but kept in a silence
room with ambient noise. The intensity of Mozart music stimulus was
65–75 dB, while the intensity of ambient noise was 65 dB.
2.4. Experimental design
As Fig. 1 shows, the animals were submitted individually to the
social approach task (SAT), elevated plus maze (EPM) test, and forced
swimming test (FST) between PND 81 and PND 90. All behavioral
procedures were conducted in a quiet room with controlled lighting
(150 lx) during the light phase, when rodents are less active. Upon
completion of the behavioral testing, subjects were sacriﬁ ced by de-
capitation, their brains were removed and hippocampal tissue samples
were obtained following the Golgi-Cox method.
2.5. Behavioral tests
2.5.1. Social approach task (SAT)
The automated three-chambered social approach task has been
widely employed as a standard test for assaying sociability in rodents

middle chamber with the dividers closed. An unfamiliar adult rat (i.e., a
novel rat of the same age and sex from a di ﬀerent litter, which was
acclimated to the small wire cage for three 10-min sessions before the
habituation period) was placed inside a small wire cage in one of the
side chambers. An identical empty wire cage was placed in the opposite
chamber. The dividers were then raised, allowing the test rat to move
freely throughout all three chambers of the apparatus over a 10-minute
test session. The location of the small wire cage and the novel rat al-
ternated across subjects. Measures were taken of (a) time spent in the
novel rat chamber, (b) time spent in the novel object (empty wire cage)
chamber, (c) time spent sniﬃ ng the novel rat, and (d) time spent
sniﬃ ng the novel object (empty wire cage) for 10 min. The test rat was
considered to be sniﬃ ng the wire cage when its nose was directed to the
cage and the distance between the nose tip and metal bars was 1 cm or
less. All behaviors were recorded using a videocamera connected to
ANY-maze software version 4.75 (Ugo Basile, Model 60000).
2.5.2. Elevated plus maze (EPM) test
The elevated plus maze test (EPM) is widely used to assess anxiety-
related behavior [ 33]. In this paradigm, an increase in closed arms
activity (time spend and/or entries) re ﬂects anxiety-like behavior,
whereas an increase in open arms activity (time spend and/or entries)
reﬂects anti-anxiety or anxiolytic behavior. The EPM apparatus con-
sisted of two open arms and two closed arms opposite to each other
(46 cm length ×15 cm width), elevated 70 cm from the ﬂoor. The
closed arms were surrounded by gray walls (23 cm height). For testing,
each rat was placed in the center of the maze facing an open arm.
During a 5 min test period, the following parameters were measured:
(a) time spent in the open arms and (b) time spent in the closed arms.
Moreover, risk assessment behaviors were also measured: (a) total
number of stretch-attend postures (SAPs), (b) protected (% Closed)
SAPs (c) number of head dips in the open arms. All behaviors were
recorded using a videocamera connected to ANY-maze software version
4.75 (Ugo Basile, Model 60000).
2.5.3. Forced swimming test (FST)
Forced swim test (FST) was based on the behavioral model ﬁrst
described by Porsolt et al. [ 34]. Brie ﬂy, the FST was performed by
placing a rat in an opaque Plexiglas cylinder (20 cm diameter ×60 cm
height) containing 40 cm of water (maintained at 24 ± 1
oC). Rats
were ﬁrstly exposed to a pre-testing session for 15 min. Twenty four
hours following the pre-testing phase, rats were subjected to a 5-min
FST. The total duration of immobility (increased immobility is in-
dicative of depression-like behavior), climbing, and swimming (in-
creased climbing and/or swimming re ﬂect an ambient increase in be-
havioral despair, indicative of depression-like behavior) was measured
using the ANY-maze software version 4.75 (Ugo Basile, Model 60000)
according to van Zyl et al. [ 35]. After the test, rats were well-dried with
towels and then transferred to their cages.
2.6. Golgi-Cox staining
Two days upon completion of the behavioral testing, rats were sa-
criﬁced by rapid decapitation, their brains were removed and placed in
Golgi-Cox solution (5% Potassium Dichromate, 5% Mercuric Chloride
(sublimate), and 5% Solution of Potassium Chromate), which had been
prepared at least 5 days earlier. Brains remained in Golgi-Cox solution
for 14 days at room temperature, then placed in 30% sucrose solution
and subsequently sliced (150- μm-thick slices) in a vibratome (Leica
VT1000S, Leica Biosystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The slices
containing the hippocampus (bregma -3 to -4.2) [ 36] were placed onto
gelatin-coated microscope slides, covered with para ﬁlm, and main-
tained in a humidity chamber for about 30 –40 h. The para ﬁlm was then
removed, and the slices were incubated ﬁrst in ammonium hydroxide
for 15 min followed by washes with dH
2O. The brain slices were then
dehydrated with increasing concentrations of ethanol, incubated in
xylene for 5 min and coverslipped with permount. The slides were kept
for at least a month before imaging under the 60x lens of Nikon Eclipse
E800 microscope (Nikon Instruments Europe BV, Amsterdam, Nether-
lands). Initially, the CA1 pyramidal hippocampal neurons to be ana-
lyzed were picked under 20x magni ﬁcation. We analyzed 6– 8 neurons
of both hemispheres from 4 sections per animal, and measured den-
dritic spine density on secondary dendrites. The spines were then
analyzed separately according to the spine type: stubby, thin and
mushroom spines [ 37]. In the ﬁnal results, mushroom and thin spines
were summed and presented as mature spines.
2.7. Statistical analysis
All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean
(SEM) and statistically analyzed using two-way analysis of variance
(ANOVA), group (maternal separation or control) × treatment
(Mozart’s sonata or ambient noise). The level of signi ﬁcance was set at
0.05. Correlated t-tests using Bonferroni ’s adjustment for multiple
comparisons were performed as post-hoc tests and the e ﬀect size of
each parameter was measured as well with eta squared ( η
2). Statistical
analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 21.0.

4. Discussion
The aim of the present study was to investigate whether musical
enrichment can counterbalance the negative impact of maternal se-
paration (MS) in early life. In particular, we studied the e ﬀects of these
environmental manipulations as well as their interaction in a battery of
behavioral tasks on anxiety, emotionality, and sociability as well as
dendritic spine formation in the CA1 region of the hippocampus using
Golgi-Cox staining. Overall, our results indicate that MS in early life
induces a variety of behavioral and neural de ﬁcits, such as reduced
social interaction, increased anxiety and despair, as well as decreased
mature dendritic spine density in hippocampal CA1 neurons.
Environmental enrichment with Mozart's K. 448 sonata could rescue
these deﬁcits in MS rats, but not in control rats.
4.1. Behavior
There is a considerable amount of literature indicating that both
positive and negative early life experiences can exert profound and
long-lasting e ﬀects on behavior, brain function and neuroplasticity
[4,38–40]. Our results agree with previous studies showing that ma-
ternal separation induces anxiety, despair, impaired social interaction
as well as deﬁ cits in dendritic spine density [ 41,42].
MS rats spent signi ﬁcantly less time contacting or sni ﬃ ng the novel
rat in the social approach task. This is in line with the results of other
studies showing that sociability in adult rats was reduced by early life
stress [ 43–45]. Musical enrichment successfully reversed MS-induced
impairment in social interest. Our results corroborate previous studies
showing that environmental [ 46] or social [ 47] enrichment is able to
enhance social approach behavior in non-stressed rats and in rats from
stressed pregnancies, respectively. It is notable that MS did not a ﬀect
the approach/interaction with non social stimuli, i.e. a novel object,
suggesting that MS and musical enrichment alters social interest in a
task-speciﬁc manner.

group, which has not been exposed neither to maternal absence nor to
musical environmental enrichment. Our results are consistent with
previous studies, in which classical music decreases stress and anxiety
in humans [ 60–63] and musical enrichment in experimental animals
induces anxiolytic-like e ﬀects in animal groups that exhibit increased
anxiety compared to control animals [ 27,64–66]. Similar to our study,
musical enrichment does not induce anxiolysis in control animals. In-
terestingly, similar results have been reported with other types of ex-
periences, such as physical exercise [ 67] or enriched environment [ 55].
We also used the FST, in which increased immobility time re ﬂects
behavioral despair and inability to cope with stressful situations, to
explore depressive-like behaviors. Our ﬁndings revealed that MS rats
exhibited increased depressive-like behavior based on the increased
immobility time and decreased coping behaviors (i.e. total activity,
climbing and swimming) in the forced swim test. Our ﬁndings are in
agreement with previous studies which have reported that MS is able to
provoke depressive-like behaviors in rodents [ 52,68–71]. Additionally,
the present results showed that musical enrichment is able to induce
antidepressant-like e ﬀects by decreasing the immobility time and in-
creasing active coping behaviors, such as climbing and swimming, in
the FST. Thus, the reduced immobility of rats exposed to musical en-
richment in the present study is indicative of resilience to depression.
Our data provide support to a previous report which indicates that
musical enrichment decreases despair-like behavior in rodents sub-
jected to early-life adversity and housed in an enriched environment
combined with auditory stimuli [ 72]. The present ﬁndings are also in
agreement with previous studies which have reported the anti-
depressant-like e ﬀects of physical exercise or other types of environ-
mental enrichment, also in early-life stress animals [ 67,69,70,73–76].
4.2.
Anatomy
The hippocampus is a key regulatory brain region implicated in
emotional regulation. It is one of the most plastic structures of the brain
and it is extremely vulnerable to environmental factors, such as stress
[77]. Thus, alterations in dendritic spine formation in the CA1 region of
the hippocampus, in response to MS experience and musical environ-
mental enrichment and the interaction between the two was also in-
vestigated. Morphological analysis revealed that musical environmental
enrichment a ﬀected spine density in the CA1 region of the hippo-
campus in the animals of the MS group, in a spine-type speci ﬁc manner.
Three main types of dendritic spines exist: the stubby, the thin and the
mushroom-type spines. The thin and mushroom-type spines are con-
sidered the mature spines, which can undergo plasticity process, while
the stubby spines are the immature spines which cannot participate
[78].
Thus, while the number of immature (stubby) dendritic spines re-
mained unchanged in all four groups, whether they were exposed to the
musical stimuli or not, the number of mature (thin & mushroom)
dendritic spines varied per group. Indeed, we observed decreased ma-
ture dendritic spines in the MS group compared to the control group,
which was reversed in the MS rats that were exposed to Mozart's sonata
K. 448. Thus, in this group (the MSM group) the dendritic density of the
mature spines was similar to the control group. We can speculate that
the decreased spine density is due to separation of o ﬀspring from the
dam. Indeed, our ﬁndings corroborate the results of previous studies
reporting decreased spine density in the CA1 region of the hippocampus
in MS animals [ 41,42]. Similarly, low maternal care has been asso-
ciated with lower spine density in CA1 cells of hippocampus [ 79].
It has been proposed that synapses develop at the same time as the
dendritic spines increase, suggesting that presynaptic and postsynaptic
elements interaction plays an active role in the formation of dendritic
spines [ 80,81]. Indeed, a decrease in spine density can be the result of
axonal loss that makes synaptic connections with these spines. There-
fore, our results on spine density reorganization suggest that MS could
disrupt spinogenesis and consequently synaptogenesis in the hippo-
campus, e ﬀects which might be related with the behavioral deﬁ cits
observed herein [ 82]. In agreement with these ﬁndings, Andersen and
Teicher [83] found that MS decreases synaptophysin in the CA1 region
of the hippocampus of rats at a post-pubertal age. Interestingly, similar
eﬀects on spine density reorganization have been reported in anxiety-
and depression-like phenotypes [ 84,85].
Mounting evidence supports the role of various types of environ-
mental enrichment in preserving dendritic spine density in various
brain regions, including the hippocampus, consequent to di ﬀerent types
of insults or damages of the brain [ 86–91]. Interestingly, long-term
human handling has been shown to increase spine density in the CA1
region of hippocampus of adult rats [ 92].
The
mechanism by which MS induces structural changes in den-
dritic spines in the CA1 region of the hippocampus is not clear. There is
a large volume of published studies describing the e ﬀects of early-life
stress on the function of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA)
[93–95]. High levels of corticosteroids not only produce diverse e ﬀects
on behavior but also induce neuronal morphological rearrangements in
the hippocampus [ 96]. Therefore, the hypersecretion of corticosterone
induced by MS may have as a result the decrease in dendritic spine
density reported in our study. Another contributor to the retraction of
dendritic spines may be the augmented glutamatergic neurotransmis-
sion and subsequent activation of NMDA receptors. This view is sup-
ported by Venero and Borrell who report increased extracellular glu-
tamate levels in the CA1 region of the hippocampus after corticosterone
treatment [ 97]. In line with this, a recent study by Masrour et al. pro-
vided evidence for increased gene expression of the NR2A subunit of
NMDA receptors in the hippocampus of MS rats [ 70]. Likewise, another
major study, using an early life stress model involving denial or ma-
ternal contact has recently reported that early life stress increases the
expression of NR2A in the hippocampus of adult rats [ 98]. Similarly,
stimulation of NMDA receptors in the CA1 region results in spine re-
traction [ 99,100], whereas administration of NMDA antagonists can
abolish the loss of dendritic spines [ 100,101]. Interestingly, chronic
administration of corticosterone to prepubertal rats has been associated
with increased expression of the NR1 and NR2A subunits of NMDA
receptors in the hippocampus [ 102]. To the contrary, running wheel
exercise, which was able to mitigate the negative e ﬀects of MS on de-
pressive-like behaviors of rats, also normalized the increased expression
of NR2A subunit of NMDA receptor in the hippocampus [ 70].
Furthermore, neurotrophins, and in particular BDNF, participate in
shaping dendritic spines [ 103,104] and may also contribute to the
morphological alterations observed herein [ 105,106]. This view ac-
cords with a study which showed a decrease in the levels of brain-de-
rived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampus of MS animals
[107]. Moreover, another neurotrophin, the nerve growth factor (NGF)
has been found to be altered in MS animals [ 108]. On the other hand, it
has been shown that environmental enrichment through musical sti-
muli leads to increased expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor
(BDNF) in various brain regions, such as the prefrontal cortex, amyg-
dala, hypothalamus and hippocampus, resulting in enhanced learning
processes and decreased depression- and anxiety-like behaviors
[29,37]. In particular, listening to Mozart's K. 448 sonata enhances the
expression of both BDNF and its receptor, TrkB, on the dentate gyrus
A. Papadakakis, et al. Behavioural Brain Research 372 (2019) 112023
7\n[p.8] and CA3 region in rats [ 28]. Interestingly, some studies have shown
that music increased the expression of BDNF only in mutants that had a
speciﬁc mutation (Val66Met) in both BDNF genes [ 27]. Similarly, rats
reared in enriched environments also show increased levels of BDNF
[17,109].
It has been demonstrated that the signaling of BDNF begins a series
of processes in the neuron, most notably the induction of the CREB
transcription factor. CREB plays a crucial role to neuroplasticity, since
its molecular pathway leads to the dephosphorylation of co ﬁlin, an
actin-binding protein that in its phosphorylated form contributes to the
disassembly of F-actin. When co ﬁlin is dephosphorylated, F-actin con-
tributes to the formation of dendritic spines [ 38]. As it becomes evident
from other studies [ 38,39], this signaling plays a decisive role in the
formation and morphology of the dendritic spines in the hippocampus
and especially in the early stages of life. This is also evident from the
eﬀect of maternal separation (MS), as this manipulation has been
shown to signi ﬁcantly reduce the density of dendritic spines in hippo-
campal CA1 and CA3 regions [ 40] and induce hippocampus-dependent
learning de ﬁcit later in life [ 41]. Thus, the possibility that musical
enrichment either counterbalanced the reduced expression of BDNF or
did not allow its reduction cannot be excluded.
A large and growing body of literature has investigated the gluco-
corticoids-BDNF crosstalk which seems essential for early-life pro-
gramming of the HPA axis and neurotrophin signaling [ 93]. It has now
been suggested that during early development, both high BDNF and low
glucocorticoid levels are required for dendritic spine formation and
subsequent synaptic integrity in the hippocampus [ 110]. MS can shift
this equilibrium contributing to heightened risk for a variety of stress-
related behavioral phenotypes in later life. It can thus be suggested that
musical enrichment induces the expression of BDNF, which then may
acts at two di ﬀerent points: initially at some part of the HPA axis and at
the same time speci ﬁcally in the pyramidal neurons of the CA1 region,
thus preventing the decrease in hippocampal dendritic spine density
and producing a physiological bu ﬀer against depression- and anxiety-
like behaviors.
In conclusion, results of this study suggest that musical enrichment
is able to reverse the negative e ﬀects of MS on anxiety, emotionality,
and sociability in adult rats. Additionally we demonstrated that MS rats
show a decrease in mature dendritic spines in the CA1 area of the
hippocampus, an e ﬀect which was reversed by exposure to Mozart's
sonata K. 448. The disruption of normal development via early life
stress, such as MS, supports the overall literature of early adverse ex-
periences leading to behavioral abnormalities in adulthood. However,
the present data indicate that these changes are not permanent and
positive/stimulating environments, such as musical enrichment, can
modulate neuronal plasticity and overcome the e ﬀects of adversity
applied during early stages of development. Our investigation into this
area is crucial to the discussion of positive environmental enrichment,
since the results indicate that musical enrichment has a preventative
eﬀect on anxiety- and depression-like behaviors both behaviorally and
neurobiologically. Finally, a number of important limitations need to be
considered. First, the speci ﬁce ﬀect
of music versus other types of au-
ditory stimuli was not investigated. Moreover, rat’ s hearing frequency is
diﬀerent from that of humans. Thus, music could sound di ﬀerently to
rats and might be to them a patterned sound. Second, in order to un-
derstand the interaction between MS and musical enrichment on hip-
pocampal structure, the present study has only investigated one mor-
phological variable, i.e. dendritic spine density. Hence, future studies
are required to elucidate other morphological changes of hippocampal
neurons, such as dendritic arborization, dendritic length and spine
morphology. Third, further experimental investigations in this area are
needed to determine if there is a causal relationship between the in-
creased spine density and the behavioral e ﬀects of musical enrichment.
Finally, on a wider level, further research must be conducted to de-
termine whether other brain areas are also a ﬀected and weather other
forms of music have similar e ﬀects.
